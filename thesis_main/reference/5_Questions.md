1\. Did any study validate the approach of using initial hedonic regression coefficients as weights for a composite accessibility index? If yes, cite the study and describe the method.

Yes, spatial economists have validated the approach of using initial hedonic regression coefficients as weights for a composite accessibility index, a method commonly referred to as the two-stage hedonic regression approach. This methodology has been specifically documented and validated in urban economic literature, such as in an enhanced hedonic regression study exploring the determinants of housing prices in Beijing using open-access POI data.

The method is designed to abandon subjective assumptions by extracting the actual capitalized value of different amenities directly from property transaction data. It is executed in the following two stages:

-   Stage 1: Implicit Price Estimation. An initial, exploratory hedonic regression is run on the regional housing dataset. The dependent variable is the property transaction or asking price, while the independent variables include standard structural attributes, environmental controls, and individual, unweighted accessibility scores calculated separately for each point-of-interest (POI) category. The resulting unstandardized regression coefficients represent the implicit marginal price (or partial elasticity) of each specific POI category.
-   Stage 2: Composite Index Construction. The statistically significant coefficients extracted from Stage 1 are normalized and applied as the actual weights to aggregate the individual POI category scores into a single, master accessibility index.

This data-driven methodology guarantees that the final composite index perfectly reflects the actual revealed preferences and willingness-to-pay of the local real estate market, because the weights are mathematically derived directly from consumer financial behavior rather than researcher intuition. Additionally, if severe multicollinearity is found among the unweighted POI categories during the first stage, researchers may also use machine learning algorithms (like Random Forest or XGBoost) to isolate feature importance to derive the weights.

2\. What decay parameter (β) values appear in hedonic price model applications in Southeast Asian or developing-country cities? Is β=2.0 within the documented range?

Yes, **β = 2.0 is within the documented range** for these contexts. In highly congested urban environments characteristic of developing nations, mobility studies frequently calibrate the friction coefficient (β) **between 1.75 and 2.0**. This elevated exponent reflects the intense difficulty of intra-city transit, ensuring that the modeled attractiveness of an amenity drops off sharply as travel times increase due to localized friction. The 2.0 value acts as a theoretical baseline historically borrowed from the classic Hansen model, which mirrored the inverse-square law of gravitational attraction found in Newtonian physics.

However, urban economic literature strongly cautions against using a fixed baseline, noting the following nuances for Southeast Asian and developing-country cities:

-   **The Danger of a Strict 2.0 Penalty:** Imposing a rigid β = 2.0 creates an aggressive distance-decay curve that assumes a consumer's willingness to travel drops off precipitously. This can drastically underestimate the capitalized real estate value of major regional amenities—such as central business districts, large retail complexes, or tertiary hospitals—that consumers are fully willing to travel longer distances to access.
-   **Decoupled Distance and Time:** In Southeast Asian metropolises, populations often rely on informal paratransit networks and navigate severe, unpredictable traffic congestion. Under these conditions, physical distance and travel time become decoupled, meaning the physical environment does not adhere to uniform Newtonian friction.
-   **Potential for Flatter Decay Curves (Lower β):** If commuters in a city like Metro Cebu show a high tolerance for longer commutes due to a lack of decentralized employment hubs, the empirically derived β will actually be much lower than the theoretical 2.0, resulting in a flatter decay curve.
-   **Category-Specific Variation:** The friction parameter varies intrinsically by the type of amenity being accessed. Consumers exhibit higher resistance to walking for daily conveniences than driving for specialized services. For instance, one study empirically derived a highly specific β of 0.00217 (when distance is measured in meters) specifically for walking trips to convenience stores.

Ultimately, while β = 2.0 appears in the literature as a baseline for congested developing cities, researchers conclude that **β should never be arbitrarily assumed; it must be calibrated empirically** using observed origin-destination flows, travel surveys, or localized property market data to accurately reflect the reality of the local transportation network.

3\. Do any studies use category-specific catchment radii rather than a single fixed radius? What radii are used and for which POI types?

Yes, **urban planning and real estate literature strongly supports the mandatory use of category-specific, variable radii tailored to the functional service tier of the amenity**. Setting a single arbitrary, uniform radius across all amenity types is considered a critical methodological error because it ignores the hierarchical nature of urban services and different scales of human spatial behavior.

Catchment radii in valuation models are typically stratified into the following three functional tiers:

-   **Micro-local / Non-motorized (400m to 800m):** This tight boundary represents a 5-to-10 minute walking threshold. It is used for **amenities that rely primarily on pedestrian access, such as convenience stores, local bus or jeepney stops, pocket parks, and primary schools**. Beyond this distance, the amenity ceases to offer a convenience premium and drops out of a buyer's localized utility function.
-   **Meso-level / Transit-oriented (1km to 3km):** This intermediate radius is applied to **facilities that attract vehicular, cycling, or transit trips, such as supermarkets, secondary schools, and standard healthcare clinics**.
-   **Macro-level / Regional (5km to 15km+):** This expansive boundary is reserved for **major economic drivers that pull populations from across the entire metropolis, including Central Business Districts (CBDs), regional shopping malls, tertiary hospitals, and major transit terminals**. Bounding these high-order amenities at a smaller radius (like 1km) would artificially truncate their massive economic pull and erase their capitalized effect on property values despite their broader influence.

4\. What documented limitations of OHANA or similar equity-mapping frameworks are identified when applied to property valuation at the urban level?

The application of the Project OHANA framework—or any similar normative equity-mapping model—to an urban property valuation study introduces severe methodological and theoretical flaws. The documented limitations of using such frameworks for hedonic pricing include:

-   **Normative vs. Positive Economic Objectives:** This is the most profound theoretical limitation. Equity mapping relies on normative economics, aiming to identify societal deficits and assess where public services *should* be to achieve social justice. Conversely, hedonic property valuation is an exercise in positive economics. It must measure the actual, ruthless market behavior of consumers and their revealed willingness to pay for proximity. While an equity model might assume all citizens should have access to a public clinic, a valuation model must objectively reflect if the local real estate market actually places a financial premium on that clinic or completely ignores it in favor of private commercial hubs.
-   **Severe Spatial Aggregation and the MAUP Problem:** Frameworks like OHANA generate accessibility heatmaps by dividing regions into massive 1km x 1km spatial grids, calculating accessibility solely from the centroid of each grid. In real estate, the price gradient is hyper-localized; the premium difference between a corner lot and an interior lot is critical. Using massive grids artificially smooths the data and neutralizes the micro-neighborhood spatial heterogeneity required for a hedonic model. This triggers a statistical bias known as the Modifiable Areal Unit Problem (MAUP), rendering the resulting coefficients statistically meaningless for accurate, parcel-level valuation.
-   **Ignoring Network Friction:** Equity frameworks frequently utilize computationally inexpensive great-circle (Euclidean) distances. By assuming consumers travel in unimpeded straight lines, this method completely ignores complex urban topography, uncrossable highways, and severe road network congestion. Euclidean metrics have a notoriously weak connection to actual consumer perception and introduce massive systematic errors into pricing models.
-   **Assumed Category Weights:** OHANA and generalized mapping projects often employ unweighted counts or assume equal importance across all amenity categories. In property valuation, assigning assumed or equal weights violates the fundamental utility postulates of economic theory, because a major hospital does not yield the exact same marginal utility to a homebuyer as a local convenience store. Valuation models require weights to be empirically derived through implicit prices to reflect true market preferences.
-   **Use of a Fixed Decay Parameter:** Equity models generally rely on a fixed theoretical baseline for their distance-decay function (such as a Newtonian β = 2.0). Congested urban environments require a variable or empirically derived impedance factor to accurately capture how real-world travel behavior and local transport infrastructure dictate a buyer's willingness to travel.

  

5\. Is there any published custom accessibility scoring model from the Philippines or Southeast Asia designed specifically for property valuation — not equity mapping?

Yes, rigorous urban economic studies across the Philippines and broader Southeast Asia frequently build **custom, context-specific accessibility models specifically designed for property valuation** rather than equity mapping. Applying generic or Western accessibility assumptions to these markets frequently yields spurious results, making highly localized model specifications the standard practice.

Notable empirical studies from the **Philippines** include:

-   **Metro Manila (Tiglao and Rivera, 2006):** Grounding their work explicitly in land market theory, these researchers utilized hedonic models to establish that accessibility—specifically the travel friction associated with the commute to work—functions as the "central dogma" determining urban residential location choices and zonal land valuation. Their models demonstrated that households seek to maximize utility through monetary and time savings, which rapidly capitalize into land values.
-   **Cebu City (Agosto):** Providing critical local context for deriving category weights, this study analyzed 31 distinct determinants of land value using primary survey data and Bureau of Internal Revenue (BIR) zonal valuations. Through multiple regression, the study definitively ranked **"Mobility" (accessibility to public transportation) and "Livability" (proximity to open space and neighborhood facilities) as the absolute primary drivers of residential value** in the local Cebu market.

Across **broader Southeast Asia**, researchers have similarly utilized custom scoring frameworks to evaluate localized property markets:

-   **Jakarta, Indonesia:** To assess the land value capture potential of the Jakarta LRT system, researchers rejected standard global models in favor of **Mixed Geographically Weighted Regression (MGWR)** to detect both corridor-wide trends and highly localized price premiums. Crucially, they measured points of interest (like hospitals and parks) using **network centrality and topological integration** rather than basic circular proximity, proving that the structural layout of the transport network dictates value just as much as physical distance.
-   **South Tangerang City, Indonesia:** A localized hedonic pricing model highlighted the danger of assuming uniform weights for all amenities. The study revealed massive spatial heterogeneity: while distance to commuter rail stations, public parks, and the CBD significantly affected property values, **proximity to malls and hospitals showed absolutely no partial effect on residential values** in that specific market.
-   **Bangkok, Thailand:** A linear hedonic price model estimating condominium values found that accessibility contributed to more than half of a property's total value. Specifically, mass transit and domestic transportation accessibility were assigned massive empirical weights—**contributing to 51.69% of the total model weight**—confirming that transit access eclipses almost all other locational amenities in heavily congested Southeast Asian corridors.
