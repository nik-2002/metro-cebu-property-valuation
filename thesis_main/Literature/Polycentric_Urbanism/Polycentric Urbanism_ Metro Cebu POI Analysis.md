# **Polycentric Urbanism and Spatial Accessibility in Metro Cebu: An Expanded Hedonic Pricing Framework**

## **Introduction: The Evolution of Urban Spatial Economics**

The rapid urbanization of Southeast Asian metropolises has fundamentally disrupted the traditional frameworks used to understand urban spatial structures and land valuation. Historically, the field of urban economics relied almost exclusively on the monocentric city model, pioneered by the Alonso-Muth-Mills (AMM) paradigm.1 This classical theoretical construct posited that a metropolitan area revolves around a single, highly dense Central Business District (CBD), which serves as the absolute center of employment, commerce, and civic activity.1 Under the AMM framework, the spatial distribution of land values, population densities, and capital intensity declines in a smooth, predictable, and monotonic gradient as the Euclidean distance from the central core increases.1 The monocentric model assumes that commuting costs to the CBD are the primary determinant of a household's residential location choice, resulting in a perfectly symmetrical bid-rent curve.1  
However, modern urban configurations—particularly in geographically constrained archipelagic regions—exhibit complex, multi-nodal spatial distributions that render the monocentric model analytically obsolete.2 The transition toward polycentric urbanism theory acknowledges that cities evolve beyond a single core, developing multiple subcenters that independently attract capital, labor, and residential development.2 The theoretical foundation for this transition is deeply rooted in the empirical analyses of urban economists. Giuliano and Small (1991) demonstrated that contemporary metropolises are defined by multiple employment subcenters, which can be systematically identified through the analysis of total employment thresholds and localized density peaks.2 These subcenters significantly alter regional commuting patterns, creating cross-directional traffic flows that defy the simple inbound-outbound commutes of the monocentric era, and fundamentally reshape regional land values.2  
Furthermore, Anas, Arnott, and Small (1998) mapped the evolutionary trajectory of human settlements, detailing how cities transition from monocentric hubs to polycentric networks and, ultimately, to highly dispersed spatial patterns driven by technological advancements in transportation and communication.2 Most critically for the discipline of spatial econometrics, McMillen (2003) utilized nonparametric statistical methods, including Locally Weighted Regression (LWR), to prove that urban property value gradients are not smooth, continuous curves.2 Instead, the urban distance gradient is more accurately conceptualized as a "bumpy" surface.2 In these environments, independent subcenters generate localized "spikes" or peaks in real estate values that operate entirely autonomously from the primary CBD.2 Heikkila et al. (1989) further solidified this framework by establishing that in highly polycentric urban regions, the distance to the traditional CBD can become statistically insignificant in hedonic pricing models when evaluated alongside the distance to the nearest emergent subcenter.2  
Applying polycentric urbanism theory to the real estate market of Metro Cebu necessitates a complete departure from single-vector distance gradients.2 It requires the implementation of advanced, multi-vector accessibility frameworks capable of parsing the friction of developing-nation urbanism, where informal institutional factors, land titling complexities, and historical zoning discrepancies continuously distort theoretical land prices.2

## **The Unique Spatial Architecture of Metro Cebu**

Metro Cebu presents a highly unique and complex spatial paradigm that is best described in urban literature as a "Linear Polycentric" model.2 Unlike cities located on expansive plains that can sprawl radially in all directions, the physical expansion of Metro Cebu is strictly dictated by extreme topographical constraints.2 The metropolis is physically hemmed in by steep, mountainous terrain to the west and the deep waters of the Mactan Channel to the east.2 This severe geographic confinement forces virtually all urban sprawl, infrastructure development, and population migration along a strict North-South longitudinal axis.2 This linear constraint amplifies the importance of transportation corridors and accelerates the formation of distinct commercial, industrial, and tourism nodes along the primary coastal highway systems.2  
An accurate hedonic price model for residential properties in Metro Cebu must, therefore, measure spatial accessibility against a matrix of multiple distinct nodes, rather than relying on a singular focal point such as the historical downtown area near Cebu City Hall.2 A rigorous defensibility assessment of the region's urban nodes identifies a highly structured, yet fragmented, polycentric hierarchy:

* **The Primary CBD:** The Cebu Business Park (CBP) serves as the unambiguous primary CBD of the region, housing the highest concentration of corporate headquarters, financial institutions, and premium commercial retail spaces.2  
* **The Redundant Employment Subcenter:** The adjacent Cebu IT Park (CITP) operates as a massive employment subcenter, serving as the nexus for the region's booming Business Process Outsourcing (BPO) and technology sectors.2 However, initial spatial statistical models reveal an extremely high spatial correlation (Pearson's ![][image1]) between the distance to CBP and the distance to CITP.2 Because academic literature and empirical models struggle to distinguish the independent spatial influence of two highly proximate, massive commercial zones, robust econometric models must likely drop CITP as an independent variable to avoid severe multicollinearity, treating the CBP-CITP agglomeration as a single, macro-level employment node.2  
* **The Northern Industrial and Commercial Node:** The Mandaue City reclamation area serves as the primary logistics, manufacturing, and heavy commercial hub.2 Its proximity to the international port facilities dictates its function within the polycentric hierarchy.2  
* **The Tourism and Aerotropolis Node:** The Mactan Central Business District, situated within Lapu-Lapu City, functions as an island commercial center.2 Driven by the continuous expansion of the Mactan-Cebu International Airport (MCIA), this node generates a distinct "aerotropolis effect," making it geometrically, economically, and functionally separate from mainland Cebu.2 The presence of terminal infrastructure, export processing zones, and dense resort developments creates an autonomous micro-economy.2  
* **Emerging Southern Nodes:** The South Road Properties (SRP) operates as a highly planned, modern waterfront commercial district.2 Further south along the linear corridor, Talisay Tabunok functions as an established, organic commercial hub, while the Minglanilla Poblacion acts as a smaller, borderline town center that services the southern residential sprawl.2  
* **Cross-Boundary Subcenter Influence:** A critical factor in polycentric spatial modeling is the phenomenon of cross-boundary subcenter effects. Naga City, located immediately south of the formally defined six-LGU (Local Government Unit) study area, possesses significant industrial power and commercial gravity.2 Urban economic theory suggests that such a "southern anchor" does not respect arbitrary political boundaries; it exerts a highly measurable, distance-decaying influence on residential property values within the study boundaries, particularly affecting the southern municipalities of Minglanilla and Talisay.2

### **The "Cebu Twist": Localized Spatial Modifications**

Standard urban economic models designed for the high-income nations of the Global North require significant econometric calibration when applied to a developing Southeast Asian metropolis.2 The valuation of real estate in the Philippines is subjected to unique local frictions, often referred to colloquially as the "Cebu Twist".2  
First, Euclidean (straight-line) distance is an inherently flawed metric in Metro Cebu.2 Due to severe, chronic traffic congestion, highly disjointed arterial road networks, and topographic bottlenecks, geometric proximity does not accurately reflect economic accessibility.2 A property located two kilometers from a subcenter across a highly congested intersection may possess lower true accessibility than a property five kilometers away along a clear expressway.2 Consequently, robust spatial models must utilize network distance (the actual distance along the road infrastructure) or, optimally, network travel time during peak and off-peak hours.2  
Second, the "Bridge Effect" acts as a profound infrastructure gatekeeper. Because the Lapu-Lapu aerotropolis and its associated tourism infrastructure are located on Mactan Island, physical distance is entirely subordinate to bridge access.2 The proximity to critical infrastructure chokepoints—such as the newly constructed Cebu-Cordova Link Expressway (CCLEX), the First Mandaue-Mactan Bridge, and the Marcelo Fernan Bridge—acts as the primary spatial determinant dictating property values in coastal municipalities like Cordova and Lapu-Lapu City.2 Real estate markets have demonstrated that land values in Cordova surged by up to 900% following the initiation of the CCLEX project, proving that infrastructure connectivity supersedes linear geographic proximity.4  
Finally, the region exhibits the "Busay Effect," a phenomenon of topographical prestige.2 In traditional flat-city urban models, property values strictly and universally decay as the distance from the CBD increases.2 In Cebu, however, the steep mountainous topography reverses this gradient in specific vectors. In highly elevated areas such as Busay, residential property values actually *increase* as the distance from the downtown CBD increases.2 This positive price elasticity relative to distance is driven by the prestige of the location, cooler microclimates, significantly lower pollution levels, and panoramic views of the Mactan Channel, fundamentally contradicting standard distance-decay theories.2

## **Methodological Foundations of Spatial Accessibility**

To rigorously quantify the spatial influence of urban amenities on residential property values across Metro Cebu's complex geography, researchers must utilize advanced accessibility indices. The most robust and theoretically sound among these methodologies is the Hansen gravity model, originally introduced by Walter G. Hansen in 1959\.2 Drawing upon the principles of Newtonian physics and John Q. Stewart’s earlier, abstract concepts of "social physics" and demographic force, the Hansen model redefined spatial theory by positing that accessibility is essentially the potential of opportunities for interaction.5  
In contemporary urban transport scholarship, planning practitioners frequently debate the merits of cumulative opportunity measures versus gravity-based measures.7 Cumulative opportunity metrics simply count the absolute number of destinations within a binary, fixed distance or time threshold (e.g., the number of jobs reachable within a 15-minute walk).7 While highly interpretable and popular for conceptualizing the "15-minute city," cumulative measures suffer from the "cliff-edge" effect, where an amenity located 14.9 minutes away is weighted equally to one located 1 minute away, while an amenity located 15.1 minutes away is assigned a value of zero.7  
Conversely, gravity-based accessibility measures are widely recognized in academic debates as theoretically superior for econometric modeling.7 By utilizing a continuous distance or time decay function—inspired by observed human travel behavior and the inherent disutility of commuting—gravity-based constructs penalize harder-to-reach destinations continuously rather than as a rigid binary.7

### **Criteria for POI Selection and the OHANA Baseline**

When computing Hansen gravity accessibility scores for residential properties, the theoretical justification for the inclusion or exclusion of specific Point of Interest (POI) categories is paramount. Methodologically, the specification of accessibility measures must be supported by random utility theory, destination choice models, and behavioral relevance.9 Researchers justify the inclusion of a specific POI type by demonstrating that its spatial presence systematically alters consumer preferences and, consequently, shifts the localized bid-rent curve.1 If an amenity reduces household search costs, fulfills high-frequency utilitarian needs, or provides substantial hedonistic utility, it qualifies for inclusion in an accessibility-based hedonic model.11  
The baseline for spatial accessibility modeling in the Philippines was established by Project OHANA (Open-source Heatmap and Analytics for Nationwide Amenities Accessibility).2 Utilizing 1 km x 1 km spatial grids and geospatial data extracted from OpenStreetMap (OSM) via the Overpass API, the OHANA framework calculates generalized gravity scores based on great-circle distances to zone centroids.2 The framework identifies six fundamental categories of essential urban infrastructure required for thriving communities:

| Baseline POI Category | Represented OpenStreetMap (OSM) Tags | Primary Urban Function |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Education** | Schools, universities, kindergartens, libraries, colleges | Human capital development; primary driver of family residential choice.2 |
| **Health** | Hospitals, clinics, pharmacies, nursing homes, dentists | Essential biological security and emergency access.2 |
| **Finance** | Banks, ATMs | Capital liquidity and commercial facilitation.2 |
| **Grocery** | Supermarkets, marketplaces, fast food, cafes, convenience stores | High-frequency nutritional sustenance.2 |
| **Transport** | Public transit stations, terminals, fuel stations | Mobility facilitation and intra-urban connectivity.2 |
| **Security** | Police stations, fire stations | Baseline physical safety and risk mitigation.2 |

While these six generalized categories successfully map basic social infrastructure equity and primary rural-to-urban migration drivers across the archipelago, they remain fundamentally insufficient for capturing the nuanced, highly specific economic dynamics of a polycentric, tourism-heavy region like Metro Cebu.2 Hedonic real estate modeling requires the integration of granular variables that reflect localized neighborhood commercial maturity, environmental aesthetic quality, and specialized economic zones.2  
Consequently, an advanced, context-specific spatial model for Metro Cebu proposes the targeted inclusion of five additional categories: Beach Resorts, Hotels and Hospitality, Parks and Green Spaces, Convenience Stores (separated from general grocery), and Gasoline Stations (separated from general transit).2

## **The Economics of Tourism Infrastructure in Coastal Municipalities**

The integration of tourism-related POIs into the Hansen gravity model is critically necessary for accurately modeling real estate in the coastal municipalities of Metro Cebu, specifically Lapu-Lapu City, Talisay, and Minglanilla.2 In these jurisdictions, tourism is not merely a supplementary economic activity; it is a primary land use that dictates the spatial structure of the municipalities.2 Urban economic literature reveals that tourism infrastructure exerts a complex, dualistic influence on residential property values, functioning simultaneously as a premium-generating environmental amenity and a value-depreciating negative externality.13

### **Beach Resorts: Amenity Capitalization vs. Externality Discounts**

Coastal amenities are highly valued public goods that drive substantial international tourism flows, yet their capitalization into the local residential real estate market is highly heterogeneous, depending heavily on the specific structural property type and the demographic profile of the buyer.15 In Lapu-Lapu City, located on Mactan Island, the coastline is heavily dominated by luxury beach resorts, dive shops, and intensive water sports facilities.2  
Extensive hedonic pricing literature from Southeast Asia and comparable global coastal contexts confirms that proximity to the shoreline and high-quality beach resorts generally commands a massive price premium.14 A rigorous study evaluating residential properties in the coastal tourist city of Zhuhai, China, demonstrated that proximity to the sea and the presence of direct sea views could explain approximately 91% of the observed variations in property prices.16 Notably, the study concluded that unobstructed sea views exerted a far more powerful upward pressure on property prices than mere physical proximity to the water, highlighting the aesthetic value of the coastline.16  
Similarly, peer-to-peer rental market research (specifically analyzing Airbnb data) in the Balearic Islands of Spain found that guests attach high implicit economic value to specific beach characteristics.15 Through hedonic modeling that incorporated interaction terms between amenities and distance-decay, the study proved that beach length, the presence of natural coastal vegetation, and the exclusivity of the coastal frontage generated massive price premiums.15 Interestingly, the study found no evidence of capitalization effects associated with beach width or sand type, suggesting that recreational scale and aesthetic exclusivity are the primary drivers of coastal value.15 In Southeast Asia, studies in Bintan Island, Indonesia, tracking a massive tourism boom from 2015 to 2020, showed property values per square meter increasing from USD 800 to USD 1,027, driven entirely by rapid resort development.17  
However, the "amenity value" of heavy tourism infrastructure is frequently offset by severe "noise and traffic externalities".14 The perpetual influx of transient populations generates severe overcrowding, localized hyper-inflation, crippling traffic bottlenecks, and acute environmental degradation.18 The negative impacts of over-tourism on permanent residential quality of life are well documented. A hedonic pricing study focused on "built" tourism resources—specifically large-scale resorts and theme parks—in the Overseas Chinese Town area of Shenzhen, China, revealed a profound negative capitalization effect.20 The econometric findings indicated that while distance to a metro station had a significant positive effect on real estate value, the distance to the theme parks and major resorts had a severe *negative* effect on price.20 Permanent residents heavily discounted properties located too close to these built tourism resources due to the relentless noise, loss of privacy, and vehicular congestion.20  
**Hypothesis Application for Metro Cebu:** In the coastal corridors of Lapu-Lapu and Talisay, the proximity to beach resorts is hypothesized to drastically increase the per-square-meter value of properties explicitly targeted at leisure buyers, expatriates, and investors utilizing short-term rental platforms.4 The recent expansion of the Mactan-Cebu International Airport (Terminal 2), which handled over 11.32 million passengers in 2024, has catalyzed this specific demand, driving up short-term rental yields in Mactan to some of the highest levels in the Visayas region.4 For high-end condominiums and vacation villas, proximity to the resort strip is a pure, unmitigated amenity.4  
Conversely, the hypothesis anticipates a stark reversal for traditional residential properties. For single-detached houses and townhomes marketed toward middle-class Filipino families seeking quiet, routinized environments and proximity to standard schools, extreme proximity to active beach resorts serves as a severe disamenity.2 The hedonic price gradient for these family-oriented properties will likely be depressed near the coast due to commercial noise pollution, the presence of transient tourist populations, and the heavy traffic congestion along the narrow coastal roads of Mactan.2

### **Hotels and Hospitality: Market Signaling and Commercial Density**

Beyond literal beachfront resorts, the targeted inclusion of inland hotels, boutique hospitality, and serviced apartments in the accessibility model serves as a potent spatial signal for urban maturity, particularly in the highly urbanized cores of Cebu City and Lapu-Lapu.2 Real estate economic theory suggests that the presence of high-end, institutional hospitality infrastructure serves to validate a neighborhood's overall security, aesthetic quality, and accessibility to prime commercial centers.13  
Extensive research into the ASEAN hospitality sector highlights that hotel pricing itself is heavily dependent on location-based premiums, with beachfront, resort-town, or prime CBD locations commanding the highest Revenue Per Available Room (RevPAR) and Average Daily Rates (ADR).27 When international hotel chains and luxury boutique accommodations cluster in specific urban zones, they inherently attract ancillary, high-end services—such as premium dining, luxury retail, and improved public utilities—which generate significant positive spillover effects for all nearby residential properties.28  
**Hypothesis Application for Metro Cebu:** In the econometric model, proximity to major hospitality POIs acts as a powerful signal of a highly "tourist-accessible area," triggering waves of tourism-inspired property development and urban gentrification.2 This perception of prime accessibility drives up the per-square-meter acquisition cost of adjacent residential condominiums.22 However, maintaining the dualistic nature of commercial infrastructure, dense hotel clusters introduce constant commercial noise, light pollution, and heavy point-source vehicular traffic (e.g., taxis and tour buses).2 Consequently, the hedonic relationship is expected to be highly non-linear: residential properties located *immediately adjacent* to large hotels may suffer a slight discount due to the noise externality, while properties situated one or two blocks away will capture the maximum amenity premium—benefiting from the neighborhood prestige without suffering the direct localized frictions.2

## **Retail Density and Infrastructure Proxies in Developing Urban Contexts**

In the developed nations of the Global North, advanced municipal datasets, precise zoning maps, and comprehensive tax records allow econometricians to precisely measure neighborhood commercial maturity and infrastructure quality. In developing nations like the Philippines, however, zoning laws are frequently fluid or poorly enforced, urban land use is highly mixed, and granular geospatial datasets are restricted or entirely unavailable.31 To overcome these data limitations, researchers must rely on proxy variables.31  
The explicit separation and inclusion of Convenience Stores and Gasoline Stations in the expanded Hansen accessibility model serves as an innovative methodological approach for proxying underlying urban density, walkability, and road corridor accessibility.2

### **Convenience Stores as Proxies for Urban Density and the 15-Minute City**

Modern convenience stores (e.g., 7-Eleven, Alfamart, Ministop, FamilyMart) are ubiquitous fixtures across the urban landscape of Metro Cebu.2 In the highly optimized field of retail geography, the spatial distribution of these lower-order, high-frequency retail outlets is strictly dictated by rigorous corporate algorithms analyzing foot traffic, daytime population density, and micro-accessibility.35 Because these corporations rely on volume to offset low profit margins per item, a store will simply not survive in an area lacking dense, walkable human traffic. Therefore, from an econometric standpoint, the density of convenience stores acts as a highly accurate, naturally occurring indicator of urban walkability and deep neighborhood commercial maturity.2  
The validity of specific retail presence as a proxy for broader neighborhood characteristics is exceptionally well documented in international real estate literature. The phenomenon colloquially known as the "Waitrose effect" in the United Kingdom demonstrates that the mere spatial presence or absence of a specific mature retail chain (in this case, an upmarket grocer) can serve as a highly reliable proxy for neighborhood health, wellbeing, and wealth.37 Neighborhoods falling within the catchment area of these specific stores commanded median home prices almost 2.5 times higher than control neighborhoods, proving that retail locations "encode" neighborhood composition.37  
In Asian urban contexts, empirical studies explicitly link convenience store density to tangible housing premiums. Groundbreaking research conducted in Taipei, Taiwan, utilizing quantile regression techniques, found that the availability and density of convenience stores within a tight 100-meter radius generated significant property price premiums.38 Crucially, this premium was most pronounced on the *lower* end of the housing price spectrum, supporting the theory that in lower-tier residential areas, where reliance on public transit and pedestrian walkability is paramount, the immediate proximity of a convenience store drastically reduces daily search costs and friction for basic goods.36  
Furthermore, convenience store density accurately reflects a city's alignment with the modern "15-minute city" planning paradigm, which dictates that essential services and nutrition must be reachable via active mobility within a short timeframe.39 Conversely, in rural areas or poorly planned urban peripheries characterized by "food deserts," the total absence of these stores indicates severe infrastructural deprivation, requiring residents to travel significantly greater distances for basic sustenance, thereby depressing underlying land values.40  
**Hypothesis Application for Metro Cebu:** Across all six LGUs under study, a statistically high density of convenience stores will reliably predict a mature, highly integrated, mixed-use, and walkable neighborhood.2 In the hedonic price model, this proxy variable is expected to yield a strong positive coefficient for residential properties. This premium will be particularly pronounced for high-density housing configurations, such as condominiums, townhouses, and apartments, by fulfilling both utilitarian needs (basic groceries) and hedonistic needs (snacks, air-conditioned environments, late-night safety).11

### **Gasoline Stations: Corridor Accessibility versus Severe NIMBY Disamenities**

Gasoline stations (e.g., Petron, Shell, Caltex) provide a completely distinct spatial signal compared to convenience stores. Their geographical placement is exclusively dictated by motorized vehicular traffic volume, specifically along primary arterial transit routes and major intersections.2 This makes them excellent, highly visible proxies for heavy road corridor accessibility and the integration of transit supply.2 A high density of gasoline stations in a macro-zone indicates a mature road network capable of supporting heavy commuter flows.  
However, the real estate literature is absolute and unequivocal regarding the localized micro-impact of gasoline stations on residential properties: they represent a classic and severe "Not In My Backyard" (NIMBY) disamenity.44 Gas stations generate acute negative externalities that fundamentally degrade the quality of residential life. These include volatile organic compound (VOC) air emissions, localized traffic bottlenecks as vehicles queue, intense 24-hour light pollution, elevated fire risks, and the perpetual, catastrophic environmental risk of Leaking Underground Storage Tanks (LUST), which contaminate surrounding soil and groundwater supplies.45  
Hedonic pricing studies from comparable developing urban markets highlight a severe, highly non-linear distance-decay effect associated with fuel infrastructure. A comprehensive empirical study focused on multifamily housing prices in Xuancheng, China, revealed a profound stigma: 86% of surveyed residents believed gas stations inherently decreased nearby housing prices, regardless of advanced safety management protocols.44 The econometric results confirmed this fear with stunning precision. Residential properties located within the closest 0–100 meter band of a gas station suffered a massive 16% reduction in housing price compared to the market baseline.44 Properties in the 101–300 meter band, and even the furthest affected band (301–600 meters), remained down by almost 9%.44 The negative valuation effect only completely dissipated at distances beyond 600 meters, where the environmental risks faded but the convenience of driving to the station remained.44  
This exact sentiment is mirrored in studies from Lagos, Nigeria, which confirm that while gas stations indicate high arterial traffic flow and commercial vitality, proximity drastically depresses residential rental values.47  
**Hypothesis Application for Metro Cebu:** The spatial econometric modeling of gasoline stations in Metro Cebu requires a sophisticated, multi-band distance approach to capture both the macro-benefit and the micro-penalty.2

| Distance from Gas Station | Expected Capitalization Effect on Residential Value | Driving Mechanism |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **0 – 200 meters** | **Severe Negative (Discount)** | Acute NIMBY externalities: VOC air pollution, groundwater contamination risks, 24-hour light/noise pollution, heavy vehicle queuing.44 |
| **201 – 600 meters** | **Moderate Negative (Discount)** | Lingering perception of hazard and diminished aesthetic quality of the immediate neighborhood.44 |
| **601 meters – 2 kilometers** | **Positive Proxy (Premium)** | The environmental hazards have dissipated entirely. The station now serves purely as a positive proxy for living near a major, highly accessible transportation corridor.2 |

## **Urban Green Space and the Complexity of Environmental Amenities**

The final proposed addition to the expanded Hansen accessibility framework for Metro Cebu is the category of Parks and Green Space, encompassing public parks, nature reserves, and urban plazas.2 In Western urban economic literature, the "standard positive amenity effect" of urban green spaces is nearly universally accepted as an axiomatic truth, with proximity to parks driving consistent, predictable price premiums.2  
However, applying this blanket assumption to the densely populated, hyper-urbanized metropolises of Southeast Asia requires a highly nuanced econometric approach. The capitalization of green space in the developing tropics is heavily influenced by the extreme scarcity of land, the varying quality of public maintenance, and crucial differentiations between structural property types.

### **Evidence from Southeast Asian Megacities**

Recent empirical research utilizing advanced machine learning techniques, such as Google Street View imagery paired with semantic segmentation models (PSPNet) to calculate a "Green View Index" (GVI) in Metro Manila, demonstrates a positive, yet highly context-dependent, relationship between visible greenery and housing prices.50 Across the entire National Capital Region (NCR), the study confirmed a baseline positive association: every 0.01 unit rise in the average GVI within a 400-meter radius of a property correlated to an approximately 2.65% increase in the housing price.50  
However, when disaggregated by urban topology using Local Climate Zones (LCZs), the effect proved highly heterogeneous. The economic premium for greenery was massive in highly urbanized, ultra-dense financial centers (e.g., Makati), where curated green space acts as an exclusive "prestige factor" for the high-end market.50 The premium was also significant in open mid-rise and low-rise zones.50 Conversely, in compact, highly dense, or informal mixed-built zones (LCZ 1-3 and 7-9), the impact of greenery became statistically insignificant, or even slightly negative.50 In poorer peripheral areas, "greenery" often does not represent a manicured park; rather, it equates to unmanaged, overgrown vacant lots that signal urban decay and attract pests, failing to translate into any property price premium.50  
A separate, exhaustive hedonic price study conducted across the five administrative cities of Jakarta, Indonesia, reinforced this immense complexity.51 The Jakarta study found that massive urban forests and large public parks successfully increased regional land prices by an impressive 17.1% and 9.2%, respectively.51 (Conversely, proximity to cemeteries severely depressed land values by 15% to 37.6% 51).  
Crucially, however, the Jakarta study found absolutely *no* statistically significant relationship between land prices and public parks within the immediate 0–500 meter micro-radius.51 The positive capitalization effect only materialized within a designated "beneficiary impact zone" of 0.5 to 2.0 kilometers away from the park.51

### **The "Nuisance Effect" and Property Type Disparities**

The bizarre lack of immediate proximity premiums (the 0-500m dead zone) in Southeast Asian cities is directly attributable to the localized externalities of public parks in highly dense environments. A comprehensive study analyzing the net impact of green spaces on non-landed private residential properties (high-rise condominiums) in land-scarce Singapore provides a critical counter-narrative to standard Western green space theories.52  
The Singapore study analyzed data from 2014 to 2018 and found that, contrary to all theoretical expectations, housing units located within a tight 400-meter radius of Park Connector Networks (PCNs) and public parks actually faced a severe *price discount* of 6.02% and 7.15%, respectively.52 Properties within 400 meters of *both* amenities suffered a compounded discount of 6.76%.52  
This discount is driven by two primary factors that are highly relevant to the property market of Metro Cebu:

1. **Overlapping Amenities and Substitution:** High-end private condominiums generally internalize their green spaces to maintain exclusivity.52 They offer private, highly curated sky-gardens, infinity pools, and secure recreational decks.52 Because the residents already pay high common area maintenance fees for superior private greenery, the marginal utility of an adjacent, crowded public park is practically zero. It provides no added value to their specific lifestyle.52  
2. **The Nuisance Externality (Noise and Congestion):** Public parks in hyper-dense Asian cities serve as the sole outlet for thousands of residents. They are highly active communal platforms featuring loud children's playgrounds, intense sports activities, early-morning aerobics groups, and dense vendor congestion.52 For residential properties located immediately adjacent to the park boundary, this translates into severe, persistent noise pollution, loss of privacy, and increased foot traffic from non-residents, ultimately driving down the property's desirability.52

**Hypothesis Application for Metro Cebu:** Given the empirical evidence from Manila, Jakarta, and Singapore, the hypothesis dictates that the hedonic price impact of parks and green spaces in Metro Cebu will fiercely bifurcate based strictly on structural property type and distance bands.2

* **Condominiums (Cebu City, IT Park, Lapu-Lapu):** Proximity to public plazas and active city parks will likely yield insignificant or outright negative coefficients.52 Condominiums provide substitute private amenities and suffer disproportionately from ground-level noise and congestion.52  
* **Single-Detached Houses (Talisay, Minglanilla, Consolacion):** In the more suburban or peripheral nodes, proximity to nature reserves and curated subdivision parks will yield a strong positive amenity effect.51 However, to avoid the nuisance effect, the maximum premium will likely be found in properties situated just outside the immediate noise radius—the 500m to 1km "sweet spot" identified in Jakarta.51  
* **Vacant Lots:** Vacant residential and commercial lots located near green spaces will command the highest relative premiums. Speculative developers price in the future potential of building high-value, eco-conscious residential units or commercial spaces that can leverage the aesthetic views without suffering the immediate residential noise complaints.58

## **Strategic Implications and Conclusion**

The rigorous application of polycentric urbanism theory to the rapidly expanding metropolis of Metro Cebu requires a profound recalibration of traditional spatial econometrics. The region simply cannot be modeled through simplistic Euclidean distances radiating from a theoretical monocentric core. Metro Cebu's extreme geographical constraints mandate a linear polycentric framework, where true accessibility is dictated by bridge infrastructure chokepoints, topographical elevation, and severe network travel times.  
Expanding the Hansen gravity accessibility model beyond basic civic infrastructure to include tourism, lifestyle, and retail proxies is vital for capturing the empirical realities of the Philippine real estate market. In the coastal municipalities of Lapu-Lapu, Talisay, and Minglanilla, beach resorts and high-end hotels act as dual-edged economic swords. They capitalize massive property premiums for the leisure, expatriate, and short-term rental markets while simultaneously generating severe noise and traffic externalities that actively degrade the utility of traditional, family-oriented residential properties.  
Furthermore, in developing urban environments characterized by fluid zoning and heterogeneous land use, the spatial density of convenience stores serves as an unparalleled, naturally occurring proxy for urban walkability, 15-minute city alignment, and deep commercial maturity. Conversely, the placement of gasoline stations forces econometric models to mathematically balance the macro-level benefits of living near a major road corridor against the severe, micro-level environmental and health penalties inflicted upon immediately adjacent properties.  
Finally, the valuation of urban green spaces in Southeast Asia shatters Western assumptions. While parks increase macro-regional land values, their immediate proximity often serves as a nuisance externality for private condominiums due to overlapping amenities and intense communal congestion, demonstrating that in Metro Cebu, property type determines whether an amenity is a blessing or a curse. By mathematically integrating these highly localized POIs, non-linear distance-decay effects, and structural property-type variations, urban planners, developers, and government agencies can construct a highly accurate, predictive hedonic pricing model tailored specifically to the unique, evolving spatial dynamics of Metro Cebu.
