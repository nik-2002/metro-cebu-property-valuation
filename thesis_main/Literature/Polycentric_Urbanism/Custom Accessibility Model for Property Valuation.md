# **Custom Spatial Accessibility Scoring Models for Residential Property Valuation: Methodological Foundations and Application to Metro Cebu**

## **The Spatial Economics of Urban Accessibility**

In the sophisticated domain of urban economics, regional planning, and real estate valuation, spatial accessibility operates as a fundamental determinant of property value. Grounded in the foundational bid-rent theories of early urban economists and the hedonic pricing model formulated by Rosen in 1974, the capitalized market value of a residential property is universally understood as the sum of the implicit prices of its individual structural, environmental, and locational attributes.1 Among these diverse attributes, locational characteristics—specifically the ease, efficiency, and cost with which a resident can access employment centers, commercial amenities, and social infrastructure—are historically the most complex to quantify with high statistical accuracy. The complexities of measuring location are heavily amplified in the rapidly urbanizing and geographically constrained metropolises of developing countries.  
In metropolitan areas such as Metro Cebu in the Philippines, explosive demographic growth, fragmented infrastructure development, and severe traffic congestion have created highly heterogeneous spatial markets where the traditional assumptions of frictionless travel completely break down.3 To measure accessibility in such environments, standardized indices are frequently employed. Prominent among these is the Philippine Project OHANA (Open-source Heatmap and Analytics for Nationwide Amenities Accessibility), a nationwide geospatial mapping initiative that utilizes Hansen’s gravitational model to provide broad spatial visualizations.3 While Project OHANA represents a significant leap forward in addressing the country's data limitations, its core architecture is expressly designed for normative public policy and equity mapping.3  
Applying an equity-based, normative accessibility model directly to a positive, market-driven hedonic valuation study introduces severe methodological flaws. Equity models are engineered to measure the theoretical distribution of resources to identify societal deficits, whereas property valuation models must measure the consumer’s actual, revealed willingness to pay for proximity. This willingness to pay is strictly constrained by perceived travel friction, localized market preferences, and the specific, highly variable utility derived from different point-of-interest (POI) categories.7 When building a custom spatial accessibility scoring model for a residential property valuation study in Metro Cebu, researchers must entirely recalibrate their approach to align with the behavioral realities of the local real estate market.  
This comprehensive report provides an exhaustive analysis of the theoretical and empirical mechanisms required to design a highly defensible, Metro Cebu-specific spatial accessibility scoring model. It systematically addresses the calibration of accessibility indices for hedonic price models, the empirical derivation of decay parameters and category weights, the nuanced selection of variable catchment radii, and the stark theoretical distinctions between normative equity mapping and positive property valuation.

## **Calibration of Accessibility Indices for Hedonic Price Models**

The specification, mathematical formulation, and calibration of accessibility indices in Hedonic Price Models (HPM) dictate the accuracy, reliability, and economic validity of the implicit prices estimated by the subsequent regression analysis. A systematic review of accessibility measures applied in contemporary housing studies reveals that indices are fundamentally defined by three interacting components: the type of measure (such as gravity-based, cumulative opportunity, or utility-based), the impedance factor (such as Euclidean distance, network distance, travel time, or generalized cost), and the definition of opportunities (such as raw POI counts, gross floor area, or employment density).7 The precise calibration of these three components determines whether the resulting index genuinely reflects market behavior.

### **The Disconnect Between Basic Impedance and Consumer Perception**

A prevalent methodological weakness in general urban accessibility mapping is the heavy reliance on basic, computationally inexpensive impedance measures, most notably Euclidean (straight-line) distance or heavily aggregated zonal measures.7 While these metrics are easy to generate using standard Geographic Information Systems (GIS), they fail to align with the theoretical foundations of the hedonic approach. The hedonic pricing framework relies entirely on the concept of consumer perception.7 For an accessibility characteristic to capitalize into the transaction price or asking rent of a home, the prospective buyer must physically perceive and value the benefit of that proximity.  
In a densely populated, topographically constrained metropolitan area like Metro Cebu, consumer perception of distance is heavily mediated by the physical realities of the road network, persistent traffic bottlenecks, and the availability of highly localized transport modes such as jeepneys, tricycles, or nascent mass transit options.10 Consequently, calibrating an accessibility index for accurate property valuation requires discarding Euclidean metrics in favor of network distance or travel time.7 These advanced metrics directly reflect the disutility, or travel friction, actually experienced by the consumer navigating the built environment.

### **Overcoming the Modifiable Areal Unit Problem**

Calibration must also contend with a pervasive geographic statistical bias known as the Modifiable Areal Unit Problem (MAUP).7 The MAUP occurs when point-based measures of spatial phenomena are artificially aggregated into arbitrary geographic zones, such as census tracts, barangays, or uniform kilometer-wide grids. Models that aggregate urban amenities into large grids artificially smooth the underlying data, causing a catastrophic loss of micro-neighborhood variance.14 In real estate valuation, the price gradient can shift dramatically within a matter of meters due to hyper-local externalities—such as the difference between residing directly on a noisy arterial road versus residing two blocks away in a quiet enclave. To prevent the severe parameter distortion caused by MAUP, valuation-focused accessibility models must calculate accessibility scores strictly from the individual property parcel coordinates (the micro-level) rather than from an arbitrary zonal centroid.13

### **Controlling for Spatial Autocorrelation and Spatial Heterogeneity**

When locally calibrated accessibility indices are inserted into a standard OLS hedonic regression, they frequently trigger spatial autocorrelation. This is the statistical phenomenon where the error terms of nearby properties exhibit strong correlation, thereby violating the fundamental independence assumptions of OLS regression and resulting in biased, inefficient parameter estimates.17 Modern calibration methodologies address this spatial dependence by pairing the custom accessibility index with advanced spatial econometrics.  
Researchers employ a variety of advanced techniques to isolate the pure price effect of the accessibility score from general spatial spillover effects. Eigenvector Spatial Filtering (ESF), for example, extracts spatial filters from a geographic weights matrix to remove spatial autocorrelation from the regression residuals, a technique proven effective in identifying the precise value of POIs in inflated metropolitan markets like Beijing.17 Alternatively, researchers utilize Spatial Autoregressive Models (SAR) to explicitly model the spatial lag 18, or Mixed Geographically Weighted Regression (MGWR) to account for spatial heterogeneity by allowing the coefficients of certain amenities to vary across different neighborhoods while holding others constant.20 In addition to these econometric techniques, hybrid models such as Quigley’s hybrid housing price model have been utilized to merge data from resold homes with once-sold homes to lower the standard errors of the regression coefficients.22  
The table below synthesizes the critical differences in calibration approaches between generalized mapping and advanced hedonic valuation:

| Calibration Component | Generalized Mapping Approach | Advanced Hedonic Valuation Approach | Economic Justification for Valuation |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Impedance Metric** | Euclidean (Straight-line) distance | Network distance or Travel time | Captures actual travel friction and consumer perception, aligning with utility maximization.7 |
| **Spatial Resolution** | Aggregated zonal centroids (Grids) | Parcel-level coordinate points | Eliminates MAUP, preserving the micro-neighborhood variance necessary for accurate pricing.13 |
| **Error Modeling** | Standard Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) | ESF, SAR, or MGWR spatial models | Controls for spatial autocorrelation, preventing biased estimates of the accessibility premium.17 |
| **Data Structure** | Cross-sectional snapshots | Repeat-sales or Hybrid models | Controls for unobserved structural characteristics and isolates the locational premium over time.22 |

Historically, the Hansen model and its many derivatives borrowed the ![][image9] parameter directly from Newtonian physics, specifically mirroring the inverse-square law of gravitational attraction.23 While adopting a squared decay parameter is mathematically elegant and computationally convenient, applying a universal ![][image9] is highly problematic for urban residential valuation. ### **Empirical Calibration of ![][image1] in Developing and Congested Cities**

In Southeast Asian metropolises, the friction of distance behaves uniquely. Formal mass transit networks are frequently underdeveloped, leading populations to rely on informal paratransit networks, motorcycles, and private vehicles, all of which must navigate severe, unpredictable traffic congestion.10 Under these conditions, physical distance and travel time become decoupled. The urban economic literature firmly establishes that ![][image1] should never be arbitrarily assumed; it must be calibrated empirically to reflect actual human travel behavior or derived from localized property market data.28  
Researchers analyzing congested urban networks deploy several sophisticated methodologies to calibrate the ![][image1] parameter effectively:

1. **Travel Behavior Derivation:** By utilizing macroscopic trip distribution models, researchers can estimate the specific ![][image1] that best fits observed origin-destination flows in a given city.30 Modern studies leverage high-resolution GPS data, cellular network pings, or localized travel surveys to pinpoint the exact threshold where residents stop traveling for specific services.32 If commuters in Metro Cebu demonstrate a high tolerance for longer commutes due to a lack of decentralized employment hubs, the corresponding empirically derived ![][image1] will be much lower (resulting in a flatter decay curve) than the theoretical 2.0.  
2. **Category-Specific Friction Coefficients:** The friction parameter varies intrinsically by the type of amenity being accessed. A consumer exhibits a much higher resistance to walking two kilometers for a loaf of bread than driving two kilometers for specialized medical care. For instance, Handy and Niemeier (1997) empirically derived a highly specific ![][image1] of 0.00217 (when distance is measured in meters) for walking trips to convenience stores, explicitly acknowledging the rapid decay of willingness to walk.34 The decay parameter for accessing a regional shopping mall or university will be substantially different, reflecting a much broader geographic pull.  
3. **Simultaneous Non-Linear Regression:** Advanced spatial econometric models estimate the decay curve simultaneously with the price elasticity using non-linear regression techniques. This allows the housing market transaction data itself to dictate the exact shape of the decay function, optimizing the parameter to maximize the explanatory power of the hedonic model.35

For a congested, multi-modal city like Metro Cebu, utilizing a fixed ![][image9] will invariably distort the spatial accessibility landscape. Implementing a localized, empirically derived ![][image1]—or transitioning away from the inverse-power function entirely in favor of non-linear transformations like the Gaussian Two-Step Floating Catchment Area (G2SFCA) which applies a bell-shaped decay curve—is a far more defensible methodology for capturing realistic human mobility and market capitalization.4

## **Deriving Category Weights for Composite Accessibility Indices**

A critical architectural choice in the design of any composite accessibility model is the determination of how to weight different POI categories (e.g., healthcare facilities versus retail centers versus green spaces) when aggregating them into a single, unified score. Generalized spatial mapping projects frequently employ unweighted counts, assume equal importance across all categories, or rely on arbitrary weights assigned by practitioner panels.27 However, in the context of real estate valuation, assigning assumed or equal weights violates the fundamental utility postulates of economic theory. A major hospital does not yield the exact same marginal utility to a prospective homebuyer as a local convenience store, and a model that treats them equally will produce severely biased property valuations.

### **Algorithmic Weight Derivation via Machine Learning**

Recent methodological advancements in real estate research utilize machine learning algorithms to derive optimal category weights, bypassing some of the multicollinearity issues inherent in standard OLS regressions.47 For instance, the Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) algorithm has been successfully integrated with traditional HPMs to identify non-linear relationships and rank the relative importance of dozens of spatial features.49  
In a prominent spatial study of the highly inflated Shenzhen housing market, XGBoost was deployed to analyze the determinants of housing prices. The algorithm empirically ranked the variables, revealing that "distance to the city center" and the "green view index" carried the heaviest weights in determining property values, effectively overriding traditional assumptions about the supremacy of educational facilities in that specific localized market.49 Similarly, Random Forest (RF) algorithms have been utilized alongside Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to search through massive datasets for the best performing, utility-bearing gravitational accessibility indices. This algorithmic derivation drastically reduces spatial autocorrelation (by up to 35% in some European studies) and generates highly interpretable, optimal indices that vastly improve price prediction accuracy.45

## **Catchment Radius Selection in Accessibility Modeling**

The selection of the catchment radius (often referred to as the buffer size or threshold distance) defines the maximum geographic boundary within which an urban amenity is assumed to exert any measurable influence on a residential property. Setting arbitrary, uniform radii across all amenity types—a common practice in simplified spatial analyses—is a critical methodological error that severely distorts the resulting accessibility scores.

### **The Necessity of Category-Specific Radii**

Human spatial behavior dictates that different economic and social activities operate on vastly different geographical scales. The urban planning and real estate literature strongly supports the mandatory use of category-specific, variable radii tailored to the functional service tier of the amenity.4 Forcing all POIs into a single spatial boundary ignores the hierarchical nature of urban services.  
Catchment radii in valuation models are typically stratified into three functional tiers:

* **Micro-local / Non-motorized (400m \- 800m):** Amenities that rely primarily on pedestrian access, such as convenience stores, local bus stops, pocket parks, and primary schools, are typically bounded by a 5-to-10 minute walking threshold.54 Beyond this tight radius, the amenity ceases to offer a convenience premium and drops out of the buyer's localized utility function.  
* **Meso-level / Transit-oriented (1km \- 3km):** Facilities that attract vehicular, cycling, or transit trips, such as supermarkets, secondary schools, and standard healthcare clinics, operate effectively within a 1km to 3km radius.57  
* **Macro-level / Regional (5km \- 15km+):** Major economic drivers such as Central Business Districts (CBDs), regional shopping malls, tertiary hospitals, and major transit terminals draw populations from across the entire metropolis. Bounding these high-order amenities at 1km would artificially truncate their capitalized effect on property values, effectively erasing their influence from the model despite their massive economic pull.58

### **Network Isochrones vs. Circular Buffers**

In densely populated Southeast Asian cities characterized by organic, irregular street patterns and fragmented infrastructure, using simple circular (Euclidean) buffers drastically overestimates accessibility by assuming a consumer can travel in an unimpeded straight line.7 A residential property might fall within a 1km Euclidean radius of a commercial center, but if an uncrossable highway, a river, or a gated community separates them, the actual network travel distance could easily exceed 3km.  
To resolve this, sophisticated valuation models achieve significantly higher explanatory power by using network service areas (isochrones) generated via GIS routing algorithms.12 A network-based radius measures the actual distance along the navigable road topology, ensuring that the properties designated as "accessible" to an amenity can actually reach it within the specified distance or time threshold. Studies evaluating the Pedestrian Route Distance (PRD) explicitly prove that the larger the discrepancy between the Euclidean distance and the network distance, the less accurate the valuation assessment becomes.60

## **Philippine and Southeast Asian Custom Scoring Examples**

Building highly custom, context-specific accessibility models rather than relying on generic, imported frameworks is standard practice in rigorous urban economic studies across Southeast Asia. The unique topology, transit infrastructure (e.g., jeepneys, tricycles, and nascent light rail), and rapid urbanization dynamics of these cities require highly localized model specifications. Applying Western accessibility assumptions to these markets frequently yields spurious regression results.

### **Metro Manila and Philippine Empirical Studies**

Tiglao and Rivera (2006) conducted foundational empirical work on the effects of accessibility on zonal land valuation in Metro Manila.62 Grounding their work explicitly in land market theory, they utilized hedonic models to establish how accessibility—specifically the friction associated with the commute to work—functions as the "central dogma" determining urban residential location choices. Their models demonstrated that households seek to maximize utility through monetary and time savings, which rapidly capitalize into the underlying zonal land values.62 The use of spatial microsimulation approaches in these environments highlights the necessity of addressing the specific commuting realities of the Philippine workforce rather than relying on generalized proximity metrics.63  
Furthermore, Agosto's analysis of land values specifically within Cebu City provides critical local context for the derivation of category weights.64 The study analyzed 31 distinct determinants of land value, utilizing primary survey data from real estate practitioners alongside secondary data from the Bureau of Internal Revenue (BIR) zonal valuations. Through factor analysis and multiple regression, the study definitively ranked the factors driving Cebu City land prices. "Mobility" (defined as accessibility to public transportation) and "Livability" (defined by proximity to open space, parks, and neighborhood facilities) were identified as the absolute primary determinants of residential value in the local market, followed closely by economic access.64 A Cebu-specific scoring model must heavily weight these specific categories to remain empirically defensible.

### **Broader Southeast Asian Hedonic Evidence**

The necessity of custom modeling is further validated by studies across the broader Southeast Asian region:

* **South Tangerang City, Indonesia:** A recent hedonic pricing model was utilized to assess the varying impacts of locational attributes on residential values. The research revealed massive spatial heterogeneity: distance to commuter rail (KRL) stations, public parks, and the CBD significantly affected property values. However, proximity to malls and hospitals showed no partial effect on residential values in that specific market.66 This striking finding underscores the extreme danger of assuming uniform weights for all amenities; a generic model would have falsely attributed value to malls and hospitals, distorting the entire valuation.  
* **Jakarta, Indonesia:** To assess the land value capture potential of the Jakarta LRT system, researchers rejected standard OLS models in favor of Mixed Geographically Weighted Regression (MGWR).20 The MGWR framework allowed the researchers to detect both corridor-wide trends and highly localized price premiums. Crucially, the researchers measured POIs (such as high schools, hospitals, and urban parks) using network centrality and topological integration rather than basic circular proximity, proving that the structure of the transport network dictates value just as much as pure distance.20  
* **Bangkok, Thailand:** A linear hedonic price model estimating the magnitude of Responsible Property Investment (RPI) factors on condominium values in Bangkok found that accessibility contributed to more than half of a property's total value. Specifically, mass transit accessibility and domestic transportation accessibility were assigned massive empirical weights (contributing to 51.69% of the total model weight alongside environmental impacts), confirming that in heavily congested Southeast Asian corridors, transit access eclipses almost all other locational amenities.68

## **OHANA Framework Limitations for Valuation Purposes**

The Project OHANA (Open-source Heatmap and Analytics for Nationwide Amenities Accessibility) framework is an innovative, open-source initiative designed specifically for *nationwide equity mapping* in the Philippines.3 Its stated objective is to identify severe spatial inequalities, deficits in social infrastructure, and public service gaps, particularly concerning vulnerable demographics such as the elderly.3 While highly effective as a normative tool for public policy and municipal resource allocation, its underlying structural mechanics render it fundamentally unsuitable for residential property valuation.  
The limitations of applying the OHANA framework to a hedonic pricing model are multi-faceted and rooted in deep econometric principles:  
**1\. Severe Spatial Aggregation (The MAUP Problem)** OHANA generates its accessibility heatmaps by dividing the Philippine administrative boundaries into massive 1km x 1km spatial grids, calculating accessibility from the centroid of each grid.3 As previously established, in urban real estate, the price gradient is hyper-localized. The premium for a corner lot versus an interior lot, or a property directly facing a park versus one a block away, is entirely lost when all properties within a 1-square-kilometer area are assigned the exact same accessibility score. This severe aggregation neutralizes the spatial heterogeneity that the hedonic model relies upon, triggering the Modifiable Areal Unit Problem and rendering the resulting coefficients statistically meaningless for parcel-level valuation.14  
**2\. Network Friction is Ignored** By utilizing great-circle (Euclidean) distances in its gravitational model, OHANA ignores the complex topography and actual road network of the region. Metro Cebu features coastal ribbon development tightly constrained by mountainous terrain in its immediate hinterlands, meaning straight-line travel is virtually impossible in many directions.70 A health facility that is 500 meters away in a straight line may require a circuitous 2-kilometer drive across congested arterials. Euclidean metrics exhibit a notoriously weak connection to actual consumer perception and introduce massive, systematic error terms into pricing models.7  
**3\. Normative vs. Positive Economic Objectives** The most profound limitation is theoretical. Equity mapping relies on normative economics; it assigns value based on societal need and social justice.6 It assumes that every citizen *should* have equal access to a public clinic or a government office, and weights the model to highlight where this equity fails. Conversely, hedonic pricing is an exercise in positive economics; it measures actual, ruthless market behavior and profit-maximizing capitalization.71 If the real estate market in Cebu places a massive financial premium on proximity to private commercial hubs and international schools, but completely ignores proximity to public health clinics (perhaps because the target demographic utilizes private vehicles to reach premium tertiary hospitals further away), the valuation model must reflect this reality. An equity model forces a moral framework onto the data; a valuation model must remain purely objective to the flow of capital.8  
The following table starkly contrasts the design architectures of the two approaches:

| Methodological Dimension | Project OHANA (Equity Mapping) | Custom Hedonic Valuation Model | Justification for Divergence in Valuation |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Spatial Resolution** | 1km x 1km Zonal Grids | Parcel-level (Point data) | 1km grids cause MAUP and smooth out micro-neighborhood price premiums.3 |
| **Distance Metric** | Euclidean / Great-Circle | Network Distance / Travel Time | Property buyers evaluate distance based on road network friction and congestion, not straight lines.7 |
| **Decay Parameter (![][image1])** | Fixed theoretical baseline (2.0) | Empirically derived / Variable | Congestion and travel behavior in Metro Cebu require locally calibrated impedance factors rather than Newtonian defaults.23 |
| **Category Weighting** | Equal / Assumed weights | Implicit prices derived from HPM | Different amenities yield vastly different marginal utilities to homebuyers. Regression derives actual willingness-to-pay.1 |
| **Primary Objective** | Normative (Assessing "Need") | Positive (Assessing "Value") | Equity models assess where services *should* be for social justice. Valuation assesses what the market is *actually* paying for them.8 |

## **Synthesis and Strategic Recommendations for Thesis Formulation**

Based on the exhaustive review of spatial econometrics, urban accessibility modeling, and Southeast Asian empirical studies, the development of a custom accessibility scoring model for a Metro Cebu residential property valuation thesis must diverge completely from the OHANA framework. To create a mathematically sound, economically defensible, and contextually accurate model, the following architectural choices must be implemented.

### **A. Derivation of Category Weights**

**Recommendation:** Abandon all assumed or equal weights. Employ a **Two-Stage Hedonic Regression** or a machine learning hybrid to derive weights empirically from the Metro Cebu real estate market.

* **Implementation Strategy:** In the exploratory first stage, regress property transaction prices against structural variables, environmental controls, and a suite of unweighted, category-specific accessibility scores (e.g., retail, transit, parks). Extract the resulting unstandardized regression coefficients (![][image15]) to serve as the foundational weights for the final composite index.17 If multicollinearity among amenities is severe, utilize a Random Forest or XGBoost algorithm to isolate feature importance.45  
* **Economic Justification:** By extracting the implicit prices generated directly by the market, the composite index becomes a true reflection of localized consumer preferences. If the regression reveals that proximity to a transit hub yields a coefficient four times larger than proximity to a public park, the composite score must mathematically weight the transit hub four times heavier.

### **B. Calibration of the Decay Parameter (![][image1])**

**Recommendation:** Discard the fixed ![][image9] assumption. Utilize a **Variable or Empirically Calibrated ![][image1]** that reflects the reality of Metro Cebu's transportation network.

* **Implementation Strategy:** Calibrate ![][image1] by utilizing localized origin-destination travel data if available. In the absence of massive mobility datasets, adopt parameters established in comparable congested Southeast Asian literature (e.g., applying flatter decay curves for regional commercial hubs and steeper curves for local walk-based conveniences).26 Alternatively, substitute the traditional Hansen gravity formula entirely with a Gaussian function (as seen in the G2SFCA method), which provides an S-shaped decay curve much more representative of urban travel resistance.4  
* **Economic Justification:** A generic inverse-square law fundamentally misrepresents human mobility in dense, traffic-constrained cities, artificially penalizing regional amenities and leading to severely distorted accessibility surfaces.25

### **C. Selection of Catchment Radii**

**Recommendation:** Implement **Category-Specific Network Radii** rather than fixed Euclidean buffers.

* **Implementation Strategy:** Generate true network service areas (isochrones) using GIS routing algorithms that account for the actual street topology of Metro Cebu. Assign radii strictly based on the functional tier of the POI. For example:  
  * Walkable amenities (convenience stores, local jeepney stops): 500m to 800m.  
  * Meso-amenities (supermarkets, secondary schools): 1.5km to 3km.  
  * Regional amenities (CBDs, tertiary hospitals, major malls): 5km to 15km.54  
* **Economic Justification:** Utilizing varying geographic boundaries acknowledges the reality that residents are perfectly willing to endure longer travel times for high-order goods and services than for low-order daily needs, thereby accurately capturing the distinct, overlapping market spheres that actively influence property prices.15

### **D. Methodological Framing**

**Recommendation:** Explicitly contrast the proposed positive economic methodology against normative, equity-driven frameworks early in the thesis.

* **Implementation Strategy:** In the methodology chapter, clearly delineate that the objective of the thesis is to measure *market capitalization* (willingness to pay) rather than *social equity* (resource distribution).8 Provide a rigorous, academic critique of generalized frameworks like OHANA, highlighting how their reliance on macro-grid aggregation (MAUP), Euclidean distance metrics, and assumed category weights are fatal to property valuation accuracy.3 Detail meticulously how the custom model resolves these critical issues by utilizing parcel-level coordinates, network routing, and algorithmically derived implicit prices.

By adopting this rigorous, data-driven methodology, the resulting accessibility index will be statistically robust, entirely shielded from the spatial biases inherent in normative mapping projects, and empirically tethered to the unique urban morphology and economic dynamics of the Metro Cebu real estate market.
