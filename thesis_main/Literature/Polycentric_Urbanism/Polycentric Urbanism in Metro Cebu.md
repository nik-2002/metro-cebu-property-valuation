# **Polycentric Urbanism and Hedonic Price Modeling in Metro Cebu: Theoretical Frameworks, Spatial Dynamics, and Cross-Boundary Influences**

## **Introduction**

The spatial configuration of modern metropolitan areas has undergone a profound transformation over the past several decades, evolving from traditional monocentric structures into complex, polycentric urban regions. In the context of developing nations, this spatial restructuring is accelerated by rapid urbanization, foreign direct investment, the rise of the business process outsourcing (BPO) industry, and localized industrial policies. Metro Cebu, the principal urban settlement of the Central Visayas region in the Philippines, perfectly exemplifies this transition. Characterized by a highly constrained topographical environment—where narrow coastal plains quickly yield to steep, undevelopable mountainous terrain—the metropolitan area has systematically expanded into a multi-nodal, elongated urban corridor rather than expanding outward in concentric rings.  
For urban economists and real estate researchers, understanding this spatial structure is paramount when modeling residential property values. The hedonic price model, derived from Lancaster’s consumer theory and Rosen’s framework of implicit markets, posits that housing is a heterogeneous good, the price of which is determined by a bundle of structural, neighborhood, and locational attributes.1 Traditionally, the Alonso-Muth-Mills (AMM) monocentric city model provided the theoretical foundation for locational attributes, assuming that all employment is concentrated in a single Central Business District (CBD) and that land rents decline monotonically as commuting costs increase with distance from this center.3 However, in a polycentric metropolis, this assumption is structurally and econometrically inadequate. Employment subcenters exert their own localized gravitational pulls, generating complex "peaks and valleys" in the metropolitan land rent gradient and altering the fundamental willingness to pay for residential proximity.5  
This report provides an exhaustive, expert-level analysis of polycentric urbanism as it applies to Metro Cebu, designed to inform the construction of a robust hedonic price model. It synthesizes foundational urban economics literature to establish the theoretical criteria for identifying subcenters, rigorously evaluates a proposed set of commercial and industrial nodes for inclusion as spatial variables, and examines the extensive planning and academic literature documenting Metro Cebu's polycentricity. Furthermore, the analysis interrogates the applicability of established polycentric rent gradient hypotheses—such as the Heikkila hypothesis—to Southeast Asian cities, explores the advanced spatial econometrics required to model cross-boundary subcenter influences such as those exerted by Naga City, and contextualizes these dynamics within the broader scope of Philippine urban evolution.

## **Theoretical Foundations of Urban Subcenter Identification**

The transition from monocentric to polycentric modeling in urban economics necessitated the development of rigorous, objective criteria to identify employment subcenters. A modeler cannot simply designate any cluster of commercial activity as a subcenter; doing so risks severe model misspecification and variance inflation. The foundational literature—spearheaded by scholars such as Anas, Arnott, and Small, Giuliano and Small, and McMillen—provides the theoretical scaffolding required to justify the inclusion of specific spatial nodes in a hedonic price model.

### **The Agglomeration and Congestion Paradigm**

Anas, Arnott, and Small (1998) articulate that polycentric urban spatial structures arise from the continuous spatial tension between agglomeration economies (centripetal forces) and congestion diseconomies (centrifugal forces).7 Firms naturally cluster to benefit from localized knowledge spillovers, shared intermediate inputs, and access to a deep, pooled labor market. However, as the primary CBD becomes overly congested—leading to exorbitant land rents, infrastructure bottlenecks, and severe commuting frictions for the labor force—clusters of firms begin to form at secondary locations.3  
These secondary locations, or subcenters, must be large enough to generate their own internal agglomeration economies to rival the traditional city center as viable places of work and production.3 In a hedonic property model, proximity to these subcenters commands a measurable price premium because it minimizes commuting costs for workers employed in these secondary nodes, thereby fundamentally reshaping the localized housing market.5 A node that merely provides local retail services to a residential neighborhood does not qualify as an employment subcenter under this theoretical framework; it must possess enough economic gravity to draw commuters from across the metropolitan region.

### **Fixed-Threshold Criteria (Giuliano and Small, 1991\)**

The pioneering empirical approach to objective subcenter identification was formulated by Giuliano and Small (1991) in their seminal analysis of the Los Angeles metropolitan region.6 They proposed a rigid, threshold-based methodology, defining an employment subcenter as a contiguous set of geographic zones (such as census tracts or traffic analysis zones) that simultaneously satisfy two specific criteria:

1. **Employment Density:** The zone must possess a minimum gross employment density. In their Los Angeles study, this was set at 10 employees per acre.10  
2. **Total Employment:** The contiguous zones must contain a minimum threshold of total employment, such as 10,000 employees, to ensure the cluster is large enough to exert significant influence on the regional economy and commuting patterns.10

While this method is highly effective in establishing a quantitative baseline for subcenter identification, it possesses inherent limitations when applied to regions with highly uneven spatial data, fragmented administrative boundaries, or heterogeneous topographical features. In developing megacities, fixed density thresholds may inadvertently exclude vital commercial nodes that function as true economic anchors but possess lower absolute densities due to informal land use, lack of vertical development, or heavily interspersed residential and informal populations.4

### **Non-Parametric and Semiparametric Methods (McMillen, 2001; 2003\)**

Recognizing the limitations of arbitrary fixed thresholds, McMillen (2001, 2003\) introduced a mathematically sophisticated, two-stage procedure to identify subcenters based entirely on localized spatial deviations.3 This approach argues that a subcenter is essentially a localized peak in the employment density surface that stands out against the general decline in density as one moves away from the primary CBD.  
The first stage of McMillen’s methodology involves estimating a smoothed employment density function for the entire metropolitan area using locally weighted regression (LWR).12 This non-parametric technique allows the density gradient to flex according to localized geographic realities. Candidate subcenters are identified as sites that exhibit statistically significant positive residuals—meaning their actual employment densities are unexpectedly high given their geographic location and their distance from the primary core.12  
In the second stage, these candidate sites are rigorously tested within a semiparametric employment density function. A node is confirmed as a definitive, mathematically defensible subcenter only if its inclusion as a distance variable significantly improves the overall explanatory power of the model.3 McMillen’s methodology is particularly relevant for secondary cities in developing countries, such as Metro Cebu, because it does not require an exhaustive prior understanding of the regional economy, nor does it rely on arbitrary fixed thresholds imported from Western urban contexts.13 It allows the actual employment and transaction data to objectively reveal the hidden polycentric structure of the city.

## **Evaluating Node Defensibility for Metro Cebu's Hedonic Model**

Applying these profound theoretical frameworks to Metro Cebu requires an intimate understanding of both the formal employment statistics and the severe spatial-economic constraints of the region. Metro Cebu's geography—an elongated coastal strip heavily constrained by mountain ranges to the west and the Mactan channel to the east—forces economic activity into linear, highly concentrated clusters.16 Because approximately 76% of Cebu City's land area is classified as unsuitable for high-density development due to steep slopes and hazard vulnerabilities, urbanization inevitably spills outward along the northern and southern coastal highways.16  
Based on the theories of Giuliano & Small (1991) and McMillen (2001, 2003), the specific nodes proposed for inclusion in the residential hedonic price model can be systematically evaluated to determine which would pass formal subcenter identification tests and which would likely fail.

### **Unambiguous Primary and Secondary Cores**

1. **Cebu Business Park (CBP):** As a massive 50-hectare master-planned mixed-use development registered with the Philippine Economic Zone Authority (PEZA), CBP functions as the undisputed primary financial and corporate CBD of the region.18 It houses multinational corporate headquarters, major banking institutions, and premium commercial retail spaces.18 Under any fixed-threshold criteria (Giuliano & Small) or non-parametric residual analysis (McMillen), CBP represents the absolute global peak of the metropolitan employment density surface. Its inclusion as the primary CBD distance variable (dist\_cebu\_business\_park\_m) is theoretically unambiguous and mandatory.  
2. **Mandaue CBD:** Mandaue City operates as the historical industrial and commercial heartland of the metropolis, acting as a crucial logistical bridge between Cebu City and the northern municipalities, as well as the gateway to Mactan Island.20 Under McMillen’s semiparametric approach, the Mandaue core would undeniably register as a massive positive residual separate from Cebu City, driven by heavy manufacturing, extensive logistics warehousing, and rapidly emerging commercial districts.19 It clearly passes the test as a major secondary employment subcenter.  
3. **Mactan CBD / Lapu-Lapu:** Located on a separate island and connected to the mainland by bridges, this node satisfies the criteria for a distinct subcenter due to both profound geographic friction and sheer economic scale. It houses the Mactan-Cebu International Airport (MCIA), massive PEZA-registered export processing zones (such as MEPZ I and II, occupying well over 175 hectares), and distinct tourism economic zones.19 It exerts a highly independent gravitational pull on housing markets, particularly generating premiums for expatriates, aviation personnel, and manufacturing executives who wish to avoid crossing the highly congested inter-island bridges. The airport itself provides an "aerotropolis" effect, broadcasting a unique price signal that must be captured.20

### **The Collinearity Deadlock: Cebu IT Park vs. Cebu Business Park**

A significant theoretical and econometric challenge arises when evaluating the inclusion of **Cebu IT Park (CITP)**. CITP is a 24-hectare PEZA-registered IT hub located in extremely close geographic proximity to the Cebu Business Park.18 Functionally, IT Park is distinct—it is dominated by Business Process Outsourcing (BPO) firms, technology companies, and a 24/7 operational ecosystem, compared to CBP's traditional daytime finance and corporate profile.18  
However, in a hedonic model measuring Euclidean or network distance, variables for CITP and CBP will exhibit severe multicollinearity (e.g., ![][image1]). While econometric literature, such as Wooldridge (2009), notes that extreme multicollinearity does not inherently bias ordinary least squares (OLS) estimators, it drastically inflates standard errors.26 This variance inflation causes the model to yield wildly unstable coefficients, where a minor change in the sample data can arbitrarily flip the sign of the locational premium, rendering the implicit price of proximity uninterpretable.26  
To resolve this "collinearity deadlock," researchers can employ Bayesian methods with strict priors, Multiscale Geographically Weighted Regression (MGWR) to isolate highly localized spatial heterogeneity, or Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to pool correlated locational variables.27 However, based on the spatial continuity principles articulated by Anas et al. (1998), it is theoretically sound to treat them as a single entity. Because their commuting sheds overlap almost entirely, and they represent adjacent peaks of high density, they effectively function as a single contiguous mega-center—a "Cebu Urban Core." It is highly recommended to drop the IT Park distance variable (dist\_it\_park\_m) and rely solely on distance to CBP to represent the primary urban core, thereby preventing variance inflation without sacrificing any spatial explanatory power.

### **Emerging and Peripheral Nodes**

1. **South Road Properties (SRP):** This 265-hectare reclaimed waterfront development represents an emerging, master-planned commercial and IT zone designed to pull the city's center of gravity southward.19 While historically large portions remained vacant, it now houses massive retail anchors (e.g., SM Seaside), vast IT campuses, and serves as the nexus for upcoming infrastructure like the Cebu-Cordova Link Expressway (CCLEX).19 In McMillen's nonparametric framework, SRP represents a rapidly growing, statistically significant positive residual. For contemporary cross-sectional housing data, it is highly defensible as a distinct subcenter altering southern land rent gradients.  
2. **Talisay Tabunok and Minglanilla Poblacion:** These nodes traditionally functioned strictly as bedroom communities supplying labor to the core. However, due to severe traffic gridlock along the southern corridor, localized commercial and retail activities have aggressively expanded.19 Talisay, officially recognized in JICA spatial plans as a key urban cluster, likely passes the Giuliano & Small threshold for a localized employment and retail subcenter.19 Minglanilla Poblacion, conversely, is highly borderline. It operates as a small town center and may lack the critical mass of total employment to satisfy Giuliano and Small's criteria. While it might appear as a minor residual in McMillen's first-stage LWR, it may fail to provide significant explanatory power in the second stage, making it a candidate for exclusion to simplify the model.  
3. **Consolacion:** Positioned as the primary northern peripheral node, Consolacion captures the direct industrial spillover from Mandaue's congestion. Its inclusion is theoretically justified, particularly if empirical pre-testing reveals a strong negative price correlation. It functions as a critical relief valve for northern industrial expansion and represents a distinct spatial pull.32

## **Metro Cebu as a Polycentric Urban Region: Evidence from Institutional and Academic Literature**

The characterization of Metro Cebu as a polycentric region is not merely a theoretical exercise; it is extensively documented and utilized across urban planning frameworks, government strategy documents, and academic research. Despite lacking a unified, legally mandated administrative body with executive powers akin to the Metropolitan Manila Development Authority (MMDA)—relying instead on cooperative advisory bodies like the Metro Cebu Development and Coordinating Board (MCDCB)—the spatial reality of the region is treated by all serious literature as highly integrated and definitively multi-nodal.32

### **Historical Planning and the Monocentric Paradigm Shift**

Since the early 1980s, institutional blueprints have conceptualized Cebu as an expanding, multi-centered region. The Metro Cebu Land Use and Transport Study (MCLUTS), completed in 1981 under the leadership of the Department of Transportation and Communications, alongside the Central Visayas Regional Project (CVRP) in 1983, officially recognized the metropolis as an integration of three core cities (Cebu, Mandaue, Lapu-Lapu) and a halo of surrounding municipalities.20  
The definitive shift away from monocentric planning was cemented by the Cebu Integrated Area Development Master Plan Study (CIADMPS) undertaken by JICA in 1994, which redefined the metropolis based on a 25-kilometer radius to include peripheral municipalities, projecting an integrated regional industrial strategy.32

### **The JICA Mega Cebu Roadmap 2050**

More recently, the Japanese International Cooperation Agency (JICA) published the "Roadmap Study for Sustainable Urban Development in Metro Cebu (Mega Cebu Roadmap 2050)", which explicitly abandons the outdated monocentric paradigm.20 The study models future population and employment distributions under two divergent scenarios: a "monocentric and densely inhabited metropolis" and a "polycentric and equitably developed urban area".20 JICA heavily advocates for the polycentric model as the only sustainable path, introducing the comprehensive "Urban Cluster System" designed to distribute economic activities efficiently across the coastal corridor.20  
Within this framework, JICA identifies a "core area" consisting of Cebu City, Mandaue, and Lapu-Lapu, unified by a "Green Loop" strategy that designates specific sub-functional hubs across the tri-city area, including a Trade and Financial Center, an IT and Creative Design Hub, and a MICE (Meetings, Incentives, Conventions, and Exhibitions) Center.20 Furthermore, the plan officially designates northern and southern municipalities—specifically citing Danao, Naga, and Carcar—as critical satellite subcenters.19 These nodes are intended to act as the eventual termini for proposed inter-city mass transit infrastructure, such as the Cebu Monorail System and MRT alignments, serving to anchor peripheral growth and alleviate core congestion.19 The roadmap also features a dedicated "Sub-Roadmap for Smart SRP Development," identifying the South Road Properties as a critical, distinct urban cluster requiring specialized energy and infrastructure planning.20

### **Academic Perspectives on Neoliberal Urbanization**

Academic literature rigorously corroborates the institutional push toward polycentricity. Studies detailing the "Ceboom" era (1988–1992) trace the rapid influx of foreign direct investment that actively bypassed the highly congested historical downtown (Colon Street area) in favor of decentralized, master-planned enclaves like the Cebu Business Park and various peripheral PEZA zones.19  
Researchers analyzing Metro Cebu's spatialities—often published through institutions like the University of San Carlos—observe that the neoliberal urbanization of Cebu has resulted in a defined "tri-city conurbation" functioning as the primary metropolitan core, surrounded by sprawling satellite municipalities that serve highly specialized industrial or residential functions.34 These scholarly analyses confirm that any housing market model relying on a single monocentric distance-to-CBD variable is fundamentally misspecified. The region operates through a complex, recursively structured web of subcenters that dictate real estate premiums, commuting flows, and infrastructure investments.16 The emergence of BPO hubs, export processing zones, and massive retail clusters are viewed as spatial fixes to the congestion of the old core, fundamentally validating the polycentric approach to modeling the city.34

## **Cross-Boundary Subcenter Effects: The Econometrics of Naga City**

A recurring and complex dilemma in urban econometrics is the treatment of powerful economic nodes located immediately adjacent to, but administratively outside, the rigidly defined boundaries of a study area. In the specific case of Metro Cebu, Naga City represents a heavy industrial and commercial powerhouse bordering the southern LGU of Minglanilla. While sometimes excluded from narrow definitions of the metropolitan core (often limited to the immediate 6 LGUs), Naga City boasts a population exceeding 133,000 and houses massive, regionally vital industrial assets.19 These include the Apo Cement Corporation, KEPCO-SPC and Salcon power plants, and various manufacturing hubs within the Naga Valley Industrial Park and the Naga Special Economic Zone.19

### **The Theoretical Imperative for Inclusion**

In spatial economics, administrative boundaries are largely invisible to market forces. The extensive literature on spatial econometrics, border effects, and cross-boundary spillovers provides a robust theoretical mandate for including distance-to-Naga (dist\_naga\_city\_m) as an external subcenter feature for properties located inside the study area, particularly in Talisay and Minglanilla.40

1. **Labor Market Accessibility and Commuting Sheds:** Hedonic models rely fundamentally on the premise that property values capitalize accessibility to employment and urban amenities.42 Naga City acts as a massive employment anchor for the entire southern corridor. Thousands of residents in Minglanilla and Talisay commute southward to Naga for high-paying industrial, energy, and manufacturing jobs, deliberately avoiding the severe northbound traffic congestion toward Cebu City. Excluding Naga City would result in severe "omitted variable bias".1 The model would fail to explain why property values in southern Minglanilla remain resilient and appreciate despite their vast distance from the Cebu Business Park, mistakenly attributing this value to random spatial error rather than proximity to a major external employment hub.1  
2. **Infrastructure Convergence and Land Banking:** Naga City is a critical infrastructural terminus for the region. It is the designated starting point for the massive Metro Cebu Expressway project—a 73.7-kilometer bypass road traversing the mountainous terrain to connect Naga directly to Danao City in the north, intended to cut cross-province travel times by 50%.29 The anticipation of this infrastructure is a massive driver of real estate speculation. Proximity to this upcoming infrastructure node is already being priced into real estate land banking and residential development in Minglanilla and Talisay.31  
3. **Environmental Externalities (Imported Neighbor Pollution):** Hedonic price models do not only capture positive amenities; they are equally crucial for accounting for negative spatial externalities. Naga City's heavy industrial profile—specifically cement production and coal-fired power generation—generates significant transboundary environmental impacts.37 Spatial econometrics research, particularly studies analyzing cross-boundary pollution in Chinese megacities, demonstrates that "imported neighbor pollution"—where PM2.5 and industrial emissions cross administrative boundaries due to wind patterns—has a profound and measurable depressive effect on local residential prices.43 Specifically, properties in southern Minglanilla may suffer a measurable price discount due to the prevailing winds carrying particulate matter from Naga's industrial zones. A hedonic model that fails to account for distance to Naga cannot accurately separate the positive employment accessibility premium from the negative industrial pollution discount.43

To accurately model these effects, modern literature suggests utilizing spatial lag models, spatial error models, or multiscale geographically weighted regression (MGWR). These techniques can accurately capture these external cross-boundary effects, mathematically recognizing Naga City as the undeniable southern anchor of the metropolitan economy, regardless of where the surveyor drew the administrative line.27

## **The Heikkila Hypothesis and the Southeast Asian Rent Gradient**

A critical, ongoing debate in polycentric urban economics is whether the traditional CBD entirely loses its statistical significance in predicting residential property values as subcenters rise to prominence. This premise is famously derived from the seminal work of Heikkila et al. (1989), who demonstrated that in the highly decentralized, sprawling, automobile-dependent environment of Los Angeles, residential land values were significantly influenced by multiple employment subcenters, ultimately rendering the distance to the traditional central business district statistically insignificant.27 In the Los Angeles paradigm, the polycentric structure entirely overrode the monocentric structure.

### **Applicability to Developing Asian Megacities**

The direct translation of the Heikkila hypothesis to mid-sized and large Southeast Asian cities—such as Metro Cebu, Metro Manila, Bangkok, or Kuala Lumpur—requires profound nuance. The empirical literature overwhelmingly indicates that while Southeast Asian cities are unequivocally polycentric, the spatial mechanics dictating real estate values differ drastically from North American "edge cities."  
In an extensive spatial econometric analysis of high-rise condominiums in Kuala Lumpur, models revealed that the traditional CBD still maintains profound, overarching significance in predicting property prices for the majority of the metropolitan area.49 Households continue to pay a massive premium for absolute centrality due to the concentration of elite amenities, mass transit convergence, and premier multinational employment.49 However, the study also identified specific suburban sub-markets (such as Mon’t Kiara and Sentul) where the rent gradient is completely inverted—prices actually *increase* as one moves further from the primary CBD and closer to master-planned suburban subcenters.49  
Similar findings are observed in Bangkok, Shanghai, and Metro Manila.50 Instead of a "flat" or insignificant CBD gradient as seen in Los Angeles, researchers observe a "hybrid" or "multi-peaked" rent surface. In these developing regions, the intense disamenities of the historical core—such as debilitating traffic congestion, severe air pollution, aging infrastructure, and high densities of informal settlements—push the middle and upper classes toward newly built polycentric enclaves (e.g., Bonifacio Global City in Manila, or Cebu IT Park in Cebu).6 Consequently, new accessibility metrics, such as proximity to newly constructed expressways or exclusive master-planned IT parks, often generate steeper localized price premiums than mere Euclidean distance to the old downtown.53 Yet, the primary CBD retains its status as the absolute peak of the pricing hierarchy.

### **Implications for Metro Cebu's Hedonic Model**

For the hedonic price model of residential properties in Metro Cebu, the Heikkila hypothesis suggests that the dist\_cebu\_business\_park\_m variable will absolutely remain statistically significant, contrary to the Los Angeles case. The Cebu Business Park remains the premier, unassailable nexus of capital, high-end retail, and elite infrastructure in the Visayas.18  
However, the model will inevitably reveal profound localized anomalies caused by subcenters. For instance, in Lapu-Lapu City or Talisay, the localized gravitational pull of the Mactan CBD or the South Road Properties will likely override the CBP's influence.27 To accurately capture this nuanced reality in a hedonic model, researchers cannot rely on a standard OLS regression with global distance variables. They must incorporate Geographically Weighted Regression (GWR) or Multiscale GWR (MGWR). These advanced spatial techniques allow the implicit price of CBD proximity to vary spatially across the six LGUs, permitting the model to show exactly where the primary CBD loses its influence and where a subcenter takes over.4 The literature proves that in developing-country cities, polycentric distance structures undeniably explain residential prices better than monocentric ones, not by erasing the center, but by adding essential secondary peaks to the model.50

## **National Context: Translating Philippine Polycentrism to Metro Cebu**

To fully contextualize Metro Cebu's spatial structure, it is highly instructive to examine the broader literature on urban polycentrism within the Philippines as a whole. The trajectory of Philippine metropolitan evolution is heavily dictated by national economic policies (such as PEZA incentives), infrastructure funding allocations, and the aggressive land-banking strategies of a few dominant real estate developers—factors that act relatively uniformly across the archipelago's major cities. Therefore, findings from studies on Metro Manila can provide vital predictive insights for Metro Cebu.

### **Evidence from the Cities and Municipalities Competitiveness Index (CMCI)**

Recent macro-level spatial analyses utilizing the Philippine Department of Trade and Industry’s Cities and Municipalities Competitiveness Index (CMCI) have proven quantitatively that competitiveness and economic dynamism are not randomly distributed but exhibit profound spatial clustering.21 In Metro Manila, the original monocentric core of Manila City has long been superseded by a vast polycentric network of highly competitive nodes (e.g., Makati CBD, Bonifacio Global City, Ortigas Center, and Alabang).60  
A rigorous comparative study of spatial clustering in Greater Metro Manila, Metropolitan Cebu, and Metropolitan Davao confirms that secondary cities are aggressively mimicking Manila's polycentric evolution.21 Using Global Moran’s I to measure the spatial association of the Economic Dynamism Index (EDi), researchers found statistically significant clustering in Metro Cebu (Moran’s I \= 0.236, z-score \= 2.84, p \< 0.05).21 This quantitative finding is critical: it indicates that highly competitive LGUs (like Cebu City and Mandaue, which boast the highest REDi values in the region) generate powerful spatial spillover effects, pulling adjacent LGUs (like Lapu-Lapu and Consolacion) into higher economic performance brackets, thereby mathematically solidifying a multi-nodal metropolitan footprint.21

### **The Private-Sector "City Within a City" Paradigm**

Furthermore, the urban morphology of Philippine polycentrism is uniquely and deeply tied to private-sector master planning rather than public zoning.54 Real estate conglomerates (e.g., Ayala Land, SM Prime Holdings, Megaworld) deliberately replicate their successful "city within a city" mixed-use models across different regions.54 Just as the development of Bonifacio Global City shifted the entire center of gravity in Metro Manila away from older districts, the aggressive development of the South Road Properties and Cebu Business Park are deliberate strategies by developers to create self-contained, live-work-play enclaves that function independently of the decaying public infrastructure of the older city.19  
Because the underlying mechanics of real estate financialization, BPO locational preferences, and middle-to-upper-class housing demands are nearly identical between Manila and Cebu, the empirical findings from Metro Manila's polycentric hedonic studies can be confidently adapted to Metro Cebu.34 In both metropolitan contexts, property buyers demonstrate a consistently high willingness to pay for absolute proximity to secure, privately managed, master-planned subcenters that offer immunity from the wider metropolitan deficits (such as flooding, traffic gridlock, and poor municipal zoning).30 This further validates the inclusion of variables like dist\_srp\_m and dist\_cebu\_business\_park\_m as primary drivers of value in the hedonic model.

## **Conclusion and Methodological Synthesis**

The spatial dynamics of Metro Cebu present a textbook, highly accelerated case of polycentric urbanization in a rapidly developing economy. Heavily constrained by rugged topography and propelled by robust foreign direct investment in manufacturing and BPO services, the region has irreversibly evolved from a historical monocentric port town into a complex, elongated network of employment, logistics, and commercial nodes.  
For the construction of a highly accurate, theoretically sound hedonic price model for residential properties in Metro Cebu, the analysis of urban economics literature, institutional planning documents, and spatial econometrics yields a definitive synthesis of modeling recommendations:

1. **Adopt a Robust Polycentric Distance Structure:** The reliance on a single distance-to-CBD variable is theoretically misspecified and empirically inadequate for Metro Cebu. The literature overwhelmingly supports the existence of multiple, highly influential employment subcenters. Distance to the Mandaue CBD, Mactan CBD, and South Road Properties must be included to capture the distinct industrial, aeronautical, and modern commercial gravitational pulls that totally override the primary CBD in their respective local spheres.  
2. **Resolve Collinearity in the Core via Consolidation:** The extreme geographic proximity of Cebu Business Park and Cebu IT Park creates a statistical collinearity deadlock that will destroy the stability of OLS estimators. Following the spatial continuity principles of agglomeration economies, these two nodes must not be treated as independent distance variables. They should be merged into a single "Distance to Urban Core" variable (anchored on CBP), or handled via advanced spatial econometrics (such as Principal Component Analysis) to prevent severe variance inflation while retaining the explanatory power of the core.  
3. **Incorporate Cross-Boundary Externalities (Naga City):** Administrative boundaries are economically porous and largely irrelevant to market forces. Naga City must be included as an external subcenter variable for properties in the southern LGUs. Its immense industrial base generates critical cross-boundary influences—functioning simultaneously as an employment magnet that boosts housing demand through wage access, and an industrial polluter that depresses localized land values through imported neighbor pollution. Excluding it guarantees omitted variable bias in the southern corridor.  
4. **Deploy Geographically Weighted Regression (GWR):** Because the significance of the primary CBD does not disappear entirely in Southeast Asian cities—as hypothesized by early North American studies like Heikkila—but rather fluctuates wildly depending on the localized sub-market (the "hybrid" rent gradient model), the hedonic framework should graduate from standard global OLS models. Utilizing Geographically Weighted Regression or Multiscale GWR allows the implicit price of proximity to any given subcenter to vary spatially. This will accurately map the complex, multi-peaked topography of Metro Cebu's residential real estate market, capturing exactly where the influence of the Cebu Business Park wanes and the influence of a subcenter like SRP or Mactan takes over.

By grounding the empirical variables in the robust theoretical frameworks of McMillen, Giuliano, and Small, and meticulously contextualizing them within the specific socio-spatial, infrastructural, and economic realities of Philippine metropolitan evolution, the resulting hedonic price model will achieve a superior degree of explanatory power, accurately reflecting the intricate and dynamic economic geography of Metro Cebu.
