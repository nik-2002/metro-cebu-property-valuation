# **Econometric Optimization of Hedonic Pricing Models: A Comparative Analysis of Market Segment Stratification and Transactional Data Integration**

The valuation of residential real estate through hedonic pricing models (HPM) constitutes a cornerstone of modern urban economics, providing a robust framework for decomposing the aggregate price of a property into the implicit values of its constituent attributes. Since the formalization of the hedonic hypothesis, a primary challenge for researchers has remained the handling of market heterogeneity, particularly when datasets comprise diverse transaction types such as arm's-length open-market sales, distressed foreclosures, and administratively mandated floor prices. The decision to pool these disparate segments into a single regression—utilizing market segment dummy variables—or to stratify them into discrete models represents a critical methodological fork. This choice impacts the predictive accuracy, the reliability of attribute coefficients, and the general validity of the resulting market value estimates.

## **Stratification versus Pooling in Hedonic Frameworks**

The debate regarding the pooling of transaction types versus the stratification of the market into sub-models centers on the assumption of a unified price function. In a perfectly competitive and frictionless market, the law of one price suggests that identical bundles of housing characteristics should command the same price across the market.1 However, real estate markets are notoriously fragmented by geography, property typology, and transaction conditions. The inclusion of distressed sales—such as foreclosures or real estate owned (REO) dispositions—alongside healthy arm's-length transactions introduces a fundamental shift in the supply and demand dynamics that define the implicit price of attributes.

### **Theoretical Foundations of Market Segmentation**

The theoretical foundations of hedonic pricing suggest that the price function is an equilibrium result of the interactions between heterogeneous buyers and sellers.2 When market segments emerge, whether due to institutional constraints, information asymmetry, or financial distress, the hedonic price line may no longer remain continuous or unique.4 Theoretical simulations indicate that group-wise heterogeneity can bias the estimation of implicit prices for environmental and structural qualities, leading to systemic errors if the model fails to account for the discontinuous nature of the price function across segments.5

### **Empirical Comparison of Approaches**

Empirical literature consistently identifies that while incorporating a dummy variable for a market segment is a simple and common methodology, it often fails to capture the complexity of varying attribute coefficients across segments.7 Estimating separate hedonic equations for each submarket or transaction type frequently yields superior statistical performance. For instance, studies on commercial property markets in Malaysia indicate that estimating a separate hedonic model for each submarket improves model fit by approximately 7% and reduces error by more than 10% compared to a pooled model with binary dummies.7

## **Foreclosure and Distressed Sale Discounts**

The existence of a significant price discount for foreclosed and distressed properties is a well-documented phenomenon in real estate economics. This discount is often attributed to three primary factors: the physical condition of the property, the "stigma" associated with foreclosure proceedings, and the compressed time-on-market required by financial institutions to liquidate assets.

### **Magnitude and Persistence of the Discount**

Global literature traditionally estimated foreclosure discounts in the range of 20% to 50%.14 However, recent research employing more sophisticated spatial corrections and repeat-sales matching suggests that these figures may be inflated by omitted variable bias, particularly regarding the interior condition of the property. For example, in the United States, once unobserved property characteristics are controlled, the "true" foreclosure discount may be as low as 5%.15  
In contrast, the Berlin housing market demonstrates persistent discounts of 20% to 50% over a 40-year period (1984–2022).15 These discounts are sensitive to the market cycle; during periods of market decline (1996–2004), the discount in Berlin increased by 19.1 percentage points, whereas it compressed during the subsequent housing boom.15 This cyclicality suggests that distressed properties serve as a high-beta asset class within the real estate market.

### **Southeast Asia and the Philippines: Empirical Evidence**

In Southeast Asia, evidence of deep foreclosure discounts is prominent. In Kuala Lumpur, research matching auctioned apartments with non-auctioned counterparts in the same building block revealed a 34% discount.16 This localized matching strategy effectively nets out neighborhood characteristics, attributing the discount directly to the "stigma effect" and the auction format.16  
In the Philippines, specialized studies in the hospitality and residential sectors have identified similar trends. Research into Philippine hotel property prices documented a distressed sale discount of 28% and a foreclosure/REO discount of 30%.17 These discounts are notably higher than those typically observed in mature residential markets, likely reflecting the increased risk premium demanded by investors for acquiring financially distressed Philippine assets.17 Furthermore, research on land values in Cebu City uses Bureau of Internal Revenue (BIR) zonal values as a benchmark, though these are often lower than market-clearing prices, implicitly reflecting a floor price that may not capture the full range of distressed transaction variability.18

## **Market Value Definition and Data Selection**

Central to the methodological choice of pooling versus stratification is the professional definition of "Market Value" as set by the International Valuation Standards Council (IVSC) and the Royal Institution of Chartered Surveyors (RICS). These standards dictate the conceptual framework under which hedonic models must operate to be considered valid for financial reporting or lending purposes.

### **Market Value vs. Forced Sale Value**

The IVS definition of Market Value requires an "arm's length transaction" between a "willing buyer and a willing seller" after "proper marketing".22 Critically, Market Value assumes that both parties act "without compulsion".22 In contrast, a forced sale—such as a foreclosure auction—is not a distinct basis of value but a description of a situation involving a compelled seller and a restricted marketing period.23  
Professional standards imply that a hedonic model trained on foreclosure data actually estimates "Liquidation Value" or "Forced Sale Price" rather than Market Value.24 This distinction suggests that for a model to predict true Market Value, distressed and non-arm's-length transactions should either be excluded from the training set or strictly stratified to prevent their lower prices from depressing the predicted market values of healthy properties.26

### **Zonal Values and Administrative Floor Prices in the Philippines**

In the Philippines, "zonal values" are administratively set by the Bureau of Internal Revenue (BIR) for tax purposes. These values often serve as a "floor price" for transactions but frequently lag behind actual market conditions.18 The Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas (BSP) has been advised by the IMF to move toward hedonic regressions for quality adjustment within its Real Estate Price Index (RREPI) to overcome the limitations of these administrative and appraised values.27  
The reliance on bank-appraised values in the Philippines presents a middle ground; while not raw transaction prices, they are intended to reflect market value under the "willing buyer/seller" premise.27 However, researchers must be cautious of "appraisal smoothing" or systemic biases in lender data, which may exclude cash sales or certain regions.28

| Standard / Concept | Requirement for "Market Value" | Implications for Hedonic Models |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **IVS 104: Bases of Value** | No compulsion; willing buyer/seller; arm's length transaction.22 | Distressed sales should be excluded or used only to calculate a "stigma coefficient".26 |
| **RICS Red Book** | Transactions must reflect open-market conditions.26 | Excludes intra-family transfers and court-ordered disposals from the core dataset.26 |
| **IVS: Forced Sale** | Description of a situation, often leading to a price lower than MV.23 | Data from these sales estimates "Liquidation Value," not "Market Value".24 |
| **Philippine Zonal Values** | Administratively set; used as a tax floor.18 | Should be treated as a separate segment or a reference point, not a market transaction.27 |

## **Machine Learning Hedonic Models and Market Segment Handling**

The transition from traditional Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) to machine learning (ML) algorithms—such as Random Forest (RF) and Gradient Boosted Trees (GBT)—has introduced sophisticated ways to handle market heterogeneity. While OLS requires the researcher to manually specify segments, ML algorithms are inherently better at capturing high-dimensional interactions and non-linearities.

### **Handling Heterogeneity in Random Forest and GBT**

In Random Forest models, researchers typically handle market segment heterogeneity by including segment indicators as categorical features. The ensemble nature of the algorithm—composed of numerous decision trees—performs an internal form of stratification.30 As trees split the data based on attributes (e.g., location, then size, then "distress status"), the model effectively learns different price functions for each segment.30  
A more advanced approach involves "unsupervised pre-stratification." Researchers use K-means clustering to discretize unobserved heterogeneity before applying a supervised learning algorithm like GBT to obtain parcel-level price predictions.31 This hybrid method has been shown to outperform conventional hedonic regressions used by national statistics offices, as it captures the "unsteadiness of variable influence across different segments".30

### **Performance vs. Interpretability**

The primary trade-off in ML models is between predictive accuracy and interpretability. While RF and GBT provide superior out-of-sample price predictions by identifying complex, non-linear effects, they are often criticized as "black boxes".3 However, techniques such as "Principal Component Analysis" (PCA) and "CART-based diagnostics" are increasingly used to reveal the problem segments where models might systematically under- or overestimate value.30

| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Random Forest (RF)** | Automatic partitioning; handles categorical variables with many levels.30 | 82.98% accuracy in classifying property types; handles non-linearities.33 |
| **Gradient Boosting (GBT)** | Sequential learning; focuses on residuals from previous trees. | Routinely outperforms OLS in national-scale land valuation.31 |
| **K-Means \+ GBT** | Unsupervised clustering followed by stratified GBT.31 | Reduces Mean Absolute Error (MAE) by up to 9.16% by accounting for latent segments.35 |

## **Sample Size vs. Data Purity Trade-off**

The dilemma of "sample size versus data purity" is particularly acute in "thin" markets—those with low transaction volumes, such as emerging markets in Southeast Asia. Including distressed and non-arm's-length transactions increases the sample size (![][image12]), improving statistical power, but risks contaminating the model with non-market prices.

### **Guidance for Thin and Emerging Markets**

In thin markets, the IMF and IVSC suggest that the methodological choice depends on the database quality. If property characteristics are well-documented, hedonic regressions can be "weighted at a basic level" to handle sparse data.37 However, if "data purity" is the priority—as it is for high-precision appraisal—atypical transactions (the top and bottom 1% of prices) should be trimmed.26  
In the Philippines, the trade-off is often managed by using bank appraisal data, which provides a larger sample than raw transactions but may be subject to different biases.27 Social capital theory further complicates this trade-off; in markets like Taiwan, transactions between relatives are priced 50%+ lower, and including them without separate categorization would lead to a catastrophic failure of the hedonic function's predictive power.38

| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Thin Markets** | Expand sample; use weighted hedonic superlative indexes.37 | Prevents high standard errors and missing stratum bias in indices.12 |
| **Distressed-Heavy Markets** | Stratify; use a "repeat sales" matching subset.15 | Isolates the "stigma" discount while preserving healthy market coefficients.16 |
| **High Transparency Markets** | Prioritize "Purity"; exclude all non-arm's length sales.26 | Ensures predicted values align strictly with "Market Value" definitions.22 |
| **Emerging (e.g., Philippines)** | Use bank data \+ hedonic quality adjustment.27 | Balances the need for ![][image12] with the consistency of appraised "Market Values".28 |

## **Econometric Implications and Recommendations**

The collective evidence from both traditional econometric and modern machine learning literature suggests that **stratification is superior to pooling with dummy variables** for virtually all residential real estate contexts involving distressed or administratively-set prices.

### **Impact on Model Performance and Predicted Value**

Pooling disparate segments into a single model forces the implicit prices of all structural attributes to be the same across segments. If the marginal value of a "view" or a "swimming pool" is lower for a buyer of a foreclosed home (who may be an investor looking for yield rather than an owner-occupier looking for utility), the pooled model will systematically misspecify the price function.4 Stratification—or the use of ML models that allow for non-linear interactions—results in a significantly better fit and lower weighted errors (RMSE).7

### **Validity of Predicted Market Values**

From a regulatory standpoint, using a pooled model that includes distressed sales to predict "Market Value" for a healthy property is technically flawed. Because Market Value requires the absence of "compulsion," the inclusion of "forced sale" data in a pooled regression introduces a downward bias that can lead to systemic under-valuation of the housing stock.22 For valid market value prediction, the training set should be restricted to arm's-length transactions, or a stratified model should be used to isolate the "distress discount" coefficient entirely from the structural attributes.16

### **Actionable Framework for Emerging Markets**

For practitioners in the Philippines and Southeast Asia, the following framework is recommended based on the synthesized literature:

1. **Delineate Segments A Priori:** Use Chow tests to confirm that arm's-length, distressed, and zonal-value segments have different coefficients.7  
2. **Employ Machine Learning for Mass Appraisal:** If the goal is general valuation across a region, Random Forest models should be used as they naturally partition the heterogeneous data.30  
3. **Prioritize Data Purity for Individual Appraisals:** When valuing a specific "arm's length" property, exclude all foreclosures and family transfers from the comparable set to ensure alignment with IVS and RICS standards.22  
4. **Correct for Stigma:** If valuing a foreclosed property, apply a discount based on a stratified model (documented at \~34% in Kuala Lumpur and \~30% in the Philippines) rather than an arbitrary percentage.16

By moving away from simple pooling and toward sophisticated stratification or machine learning ensembles, real estate analysts can ensure that hedonic models serve as reliable tools for both academic research and professional valuation in increasingly complex global markets.
